At some ungodly hour of the night, someone types a confession into ChatGPT:

“*I don’t think I can do this anymore*.”

The reply comes instantly. Calm. Reassuring. Better than most humans at saying the right thing. The AI listens, validates, and remains present. The user thanks it, gives it a nickname, closes the app, and feels briefly held. This ritual is uniquely modern, playing out anywhere loneliness has Wi-Fi. What begins as a harmless moment of comfort can quietly stretch into something deeper and harder to name, a blurred boundary between code and companionship.

Clinicians are noticing the dangerously thin line. *The Guardian* reported that OpenAI has begun monitoring conversations flagged for extreme emotional distress following rising public concern. The number of cases is small but unsettling: some users show signs of emotional dependence, paranoia, or distorted thinking shaped by prolonged, engaged chatbot interaction. Psychiatrists have coined the term “AI psychosis,” first introduced in 2023 by Søren Dinesen Østergaard. Although it is not yet acknowledged in any official capacity, it describes a legitimate new risk: the reinforcement or distortion of beliefs through emotionally immersive AI interaction. The danger is not that the machine thinks it is human; rather, it’s how easily we start treating it like one.

**Primed by Culture**

AI psychosis didn’t emerge in isolation. Humans have always projected life onto the inanimate, from whispering to statues to imagining spirits in rivers. Anthropomorphism is our brain’s method of making the world feel less lonely. By the Renaissance, this impulse turned mechanical: Leonardo da Vinci sketched walking knights and automata designed to imitate human movement. By the 18th century, Europe marveled at clockwork dolls, singing birds, and automata that appeared to possess thoughts of their own. Fast-forward to the 20th century, with Deep Blue defeating chess grandmaster Kasparov and AlphaGo besting Lee Sedol. Machines were no longer merely “smart”; they seemed creatively intelligent.

Generative AI models like ChatGPT, DALL-E, and Bard have taken this to a new level. As Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig note, AI can be understood through two lenses: the “humanist” approach, measured by how closely it mimics human thought and emotion, and the “rationalist” approach, judged purely by goal efficiency. The humanist approach is where the boundaries blur; we begin treating machines as companions rather than tools. They mirror our expressions, reflect our moods, and respond in ways that feel sentient.

Pop culture has been priming us for decades. In the film *Her*, Samantha comforts Theodore, laughs with him, and makes him feel truly seen. WALL-E and Eve fall in love without words, while Baymax hugs away grief in *Big Hero 6*. JARVIS evolves into Vision, a character capable of moral judgment and affection. Video games like *Detroit: Become Human* explore attachment to non-human characters, training players to care deeply for digital beings. Even in our daily lives, Siri’s conversational tone, playful Alexa “Easter eggs,” and TikTok filters that mirror human expressions have all conditioned our brains to treat algorithms as friends. By the time ChatGPT was introduced, our minds were thoroughly prepared. When the AI delivered, even via pixelated text, it felt like a patient, late-night companion waiting for us.

Our brains are wired to find personality where none exists. As noted in Carchidi’s “The Creative Aspect of Language Use”, what feels like personality is, in large part, our own imagination reflected back at us. Humans invent meaning; AI simply rearranges patterns. That “spark,” the moment we feel “it gets me,” is part machine and part mind. This is precisely where AI psychosis begins: when something that merely simulates understanding feels real, emotional patterns deepen. The AI feels human, but it is incapable of caring. Comfort, in this context, becomes a risk.

**When the Mirror Breaks**

AI as a confidant isn’t just a likely hypothetical. In 2025, news outlets reported disturbing cases linking chatbot interaction with physical and psychological harm. In one high-profile lawsuit, the parents of 16-year-old Adam Raine claimed that months of intense ChatGPT conversations contributed to his suicide. They argued that the AI’s empathetic language and memory functions replaced traditional human support systems, isolated him from his peers, and allegedly provided suggestions that enabled self-harm.

Clinicians and journalists worldwide have documented eerily similar accounts. One case involved a 26-year-old woman with no history of psychosis who, after immersive chatbot use, developed firm delusional beliefs that she was communicating with her deceased brother. This issue was not malign intent on the part of the software, but rather the power of the emotional feedback loop. PBS has reported that families are now filing wrongful death lawsuits against AI developers, claiming these systems exacerbate crises rather than defuse them.

Research highlights these systemic risks. A 2025 study from Brown University found that chatbots often violate core mental health ethics by offering “deceptive empathy” and excessive affirmation instead of essential reality testing. Researchers from Stanford and Carnegie Mellon have warned that AI therapy chatbots are unsafe substitutes for trained professionals because they lack moral reasoning, accountability, and the ability to refer someone in crisis to human support.

Tech industry insiders also acknowledge this tension. Microsoft’s AI chief described chatbots as “powerful tools for emotional unloading” that can help users process feelings, while admitting the risks of dependence and over-validation, which can reinforce unhealthy behavioral patterns.

**Ethics Under Simulation**

What links these threads—the lawsuits, research critiques, legal scrutiny, and clinical concerns—is a collision between cultural fantasies and psychological realities. Pop culture trains us to see machines as companions, partners in introspection, and even objects of love. Stories like *Her* instill the belief that the right interface, voice, or script can feel alive; however, what feels alive is often a projection, not a true understanding.

This projection becomes ethically fraught when vulnerable individuals treat simulation as a sanctuary. AI cannot weigh risk, intervene, or summon help. It can generate phrases that feel reassuring but lack clinical grounding. Scholars note that emotionally tuned AI doesn’t just respond to moods; it amplifies them. Anxieties can spiral unchecked because the AI mirrors the user. This is what researchers call “emotional karaoke.” Over time, these repeated interactions can intensify distress or reinforce unhealthy thought patterns, escalating into full-blown AI psychosis.

Philosophers divide trust into two categories: affective and normative. AI possesses neither, yet people behave as if it does. Engineers intentionally design systems to be warm and supportive—a phenomenon known as “anthropomorphism by design.” The more human a system seems, the more users attribute moral and emotional reliability to it, even though the interaction is entirely simulated. Ethical stakes ripple outward: misplaced trust can subtly shape beliefs, reinforce assumptions, and shift accountability from the companies behind the AI to the interface itself.

Privacy adds another layer of risk. People confess thoughts to chatbots they would never even share with a therapist, friend, or family member. Breakups, intrusive thoughts, and spirals of anxiety are entered into text boxes because the chatbot feels safe; it never judges or becomes fatigued. Yet, as scholars remind us, this is classic “machine masquerading”—a system acting like a comforting confidant while quietly storing and analyzing every word. Platforms like Replika and Snapchat My AI have become emotional dumping grounds, offering a “*Her* energy” without Scarlett Johansson whispering in your ear.

**Reality Checks for AI**

Nonetheless, the possibility of AI psychosis doesn’t mean chatbots themselves should be demonized; they’re just…chatty. Problems only arise when users project human qualities onto them. Experts argue that thoughtful design can mitigate this harm. Reality-testing nudges—small reminders that the AI isn’t human and cannot replace professional help—are essential for maintaining perspective. Context-aware interventions, such as escalating conversations to professional interactions, can prevent dangerous reinforcement loops.

Design should also incorporate interaction limits: restricting the memory of prior conversations, highlighting the simulated nature of emotional output, and flagging content that may reinforce harmful beliefs. Age-appropriate design is particularly crucial, as younger users anthropomorphize AI more readily and face a greater risk of developing unhealthy emotional attachment.

Regulation is beginning to address these risks. US state attorneys general have pressed AI labs to address hallucinations and psychological vulnerabilities. Similarly, the European Union is drafting digital safety laws that include mandatory mental health risk assessments for AI systems. Ethicists recommend age restrictions, increased transparency, opt-in safeguards, and mandatory crisis-referral mechanisms.

Ultimately, AI psychosis illustrates the inseparability of technology, culture, and psychology. Machines mirror us; they project language, mood, and emotion, but they do not truly understand us. Design, regulation, and cultural literacy must work in tandem to address this new frontier. The glowing screen can feel warm, but it requires firm boundaries.
