I’ve been encountering a peculiar dilemma since I arrived as an exchange student in Korea. As a Korean-American, I look like I belong in this country—and that’s the root of this dilemma. Unlike other foreigners, I have no linguistic grace period, so when I speak for the first time, the gap between how I look and how I sound can make me seem less like someone navigating a second language and more like someone who has speech impediments. While I am partially generalizing my countless encounters with Koreans so far, it did come to a head when I drank with club members for the first time.  

MBTI (Myers-Briggs Type Indicator) is a popularized personality sorting system that categorizes people into 16 types based on four traits: introversion/extroversion, intuition/sensing, thinking/feeling, and judging/perceiving. Despite originating in the U.S., it is globally known and, in particular, has become embedded in Korean social culture as a shorthand of sorts for understanding people you’ve just met. It has also made its way into dating culture and even job interviews.  

Inevitably, the question came up. When I said I was an ENFJ, a few people looked at me incredulously. “E” for extroverted? The label carries certain expectations: someone talkative, unguarded, quick to fill the silence. Generally speaking, I fit the bill. I love meeting new people, I’d rather be with others than alone, and I can talk a lot. What I was that evening, however, was sleep-deprived, guarded with my words, and mentally overworking to fit into this entirely new social atmosphere that felt super unfamiliar. I didn’t want to accidentally crack a joke Koreans would find disrespectful, but I wasn’t at all uncomfortable or shy with the others. My reservedness was unrelated to whether I was an E or an I. However, clearly that was not how it was interpreted.  

These students had known me for the length of one drink. Yet, a judgment had already been made about something I consider a core part of who I am. It got me thinking a lot about MBTI and Korea's longer history of social categorization. Korea seems all about placing people into easy-to-identify boxes and labels “just for fun,” but could this have serious consequences? In addition, why do the systems for social categories keep getting simpler even as society gets more complex? Before I attempt to answer some of these questions, let us establish some context for Korea's social categorization trends.  

To understand why Korea keeps reaching for social frameworks like the ones we see today, it helps to start far in the past. Starting from even before the Joseon dynasty, which was founded in 1392, Confucianism governed social philosophy. It was the backbone of everyday life and, unlike Western moral frameworks that centered on the self, was inherently collectivist. Every individual was perceived primarily as a part of a larger unit, whether that be family, class, or country. Social position and age were not just markers of status, but went so far as to reflect a cosmic and natural order.  

This worldview gave rise to *saju* and the use of the Chinese zodiac system. *Saju* is a fortune-telling system originating from ancient China that maps a person's fate across four pillars: birth year, month, day, and hour. It provides thousands of unique combinations that are supposed to reveal everything from ideal career to future spouse. Even now, you can commonly see professional *saju* readers lining the streets of Seoul, some even being celebrity readers whose services cost a small fortune. The Chinese zodiac, which Korea also adopted, works similarly. Using the twelve-year animal cycle you could know someone's sign and figure out what year they were born and from that, their personality quirks or future prospects. Together, *saju* and the zodiac share the same underlying logic: the self defined in relation to others, and a quick way to sort the room. Both are centered around not just your own destiny, but your place and interactions with society as a whole.   

If we fast-forward to the modern era, we can see that the impulse to categorize and the affinity for social order haven’t disappeared. This is exemplified by the popularity of blood types and MBTI. Blood type personality theory, which originated in Japan but became popular in Korea, assigned broad character traits to A, B, O, and AB types. Of course, the popularity of this theory has since been replaced by the MBTI rage. I don’t believe there is anything inherently wrong with any of this. If anything, it’s a fun way to get to know someone. But I think we should be more critical.  

Why did one offhand comment at that first dinner bother me as much as it did? Because when my clubmates said I didn’t seem extroverted, it didn’t just feel like an observation, but almost like a correction. I suddenly felt a pressure to perform based on my personality name tag rather than what felt right in the moment. Internalization can also lead to letting it become a permission slip. Introversion shouldn’t excuse you from showing up when you’re needed. Being a “T” shouldn’t mean a grieving friend needs to hear a logical breakdown of how their grandmother had a stroke. It’s possible for the label to stop describing who you are and start justifying who you've decided not to become just because of widespread social acknowledgement that you are a ZHBV. When you change the letters, it becomes nothing more than a silly characterization; and that's how it should stay, if we are to encourage only the positives from trends like these.  

There is a broader risk here too that exists beyond the individual. When a society collectively buys into the idea that people can be sorted into fixed, inherent categories, it quietly sets a precedent that deviating from your type is somehow wrong rather than simply human. Foreigners often remark on how visually uniform Korean society can feel; something as small as the near-universal haircut among young men is a window into a culture that places value on fitting in. Personality frameworks reinforce that pressure, since being different from your label feels like a social error. It risks making people less willing to be surprising, contradictory, or openly themselves. A society is richer for its outliers, and these systems of categorization, when taken too seriously, can make outliers feel like mistakes. Does being extroverted mean you need to be loud around people you don’t like? When dealing with death, do you turn to someone who understands your situation regardless of MBTI or someone whose MBTI is feeling? When preparing for an interview as a sales representative, should you lie and say you're an E? Of course we are all human and the answer to all those questions should be that we can be who we are without worry.  

Another rising trend is *teto-egen*, which sorts people into two types based on “gender energy”: *teto* (testosterone-type) for assertive and competitive, *egen* (estrogen-type) for warm and communal. Unlike every trend that came before it, *teto-egen* isn’t about compatibility or fate or even personality; it’s purely about labeling, and the label it reaches for is gender. Some might point to the *egen-guy* or *teto-girl* as proof that the system subverts norms, but the *egen-guy* is just as often a punchline as he is a personality type, and the fact that *liking movies* needs a special category says everything. The trend doesn’t emerge from nowhere. It borrows the cultural weight of Korea's preexisting gender expectations and repackages them as something playful and low-stakes. If the risk with MBTI is that individuals stop growing, the risk with *teto-egen* is greater, and nudges gender itself back toward a binary at exactly the moment that binary was beginning to loosen. People should be aware of the origins of these trends and the inadvertent issues they could imply, as with all trends.   

Contrary to a collectivist society is an individualistic society. The U.S., where I’m from, exemplifies this. Personality traits are not labels but optional accessories. You take the quiz, laugh about it once or twice to someone, and move on because there is an underlying assumption that you can always redefine yourself. It’s not just acceptable but rather admirable in American culture to change. I love asking people's MBTI but would never use it to predict their actions or give it any more significance than as a conversation starter. On the other hand, all these frameworks from *saju* to MBTI embody the very human need to be understood quickly and to understand others the same way. From a positive perspective, Korea, it seems, has been refining these cunning social shortcuts for centuries.  

My bet is that the next trend after this one is already forming in a KakaoTalk group chat somewhere, unbeknownst to its impending viral potential. A new icebreaker, a reason to laugh, a door into knowing someone; as long as we don’t let it be the last word on who someone is, there are real positives to these quirky systems. The most interesting parts of getting to know people are always the parts that don’t fit the box, like the fondness in seeing your initially quiet friend turn into the one who’s always the loudest in the room, or the relief in finding out your “T” friend actually has the best emotional advice for your situation because they went through the same thing the previous year. Maybe that’s the real gift these frameworks can give us: not an easily labeled map of who someone is, but rather a starting point from which we get to be surprised.
