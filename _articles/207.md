2026 has dawned upon us. We are truly at the height of an “era of great taste.” Terms like “aesthetic sense” and “style” have moved beyond trendy expressions to circulate as markers of cultural literacy. Brands proclaim, “we respect your taste,” magazines insist that “taste is attitude,” and individuals display their tastes as if they were personal résumés. Having *good taste* now seems to be a virtue expected of the modern individual. Yet a question remains: what exactly is this “taste” that we obsess over, and why has it become so important at this particular moment?

Taste is a habit of value judgment. Humans constantly choose among values that are difficult to quantify. Between beauty and utility, novelty and familiarity, safety and risk, each individual develops a direction in which their scale repeatedly tilts. That direction—the tendency in how one chooses—is taste. Within this context, taste is less a catalog of “what one enjoys” than a weighting of “what one values.”

Such taste takes shape through language. Choice often begins with a vague attraction, and when that attraction is not ignored, taste begins to reveal itself. One articulates why a choice was made, and that language becomes the criterion for the next choice. As this cycle of choosing and articulation repeats, the boundaries of the self expand. Taste converts reactions to the world into interpretation; those interpretations continually renew the individual, and taste ultimately becomes a lifestyle through which the self grows and evolves.

So why has taste become such a prominent social trend today? It is because labor and utility can no longer convincingly serve as the foundations for defining the self. Once, work functioned as proof of one’s existence. Wages and performance provided the sense that one was needed in the world. But in an age of overexposure and constant comparison, this is no longer the case. Individual contributions become relativized, and AI further erodes the symbolic value of labor and productivity. In a time when people increasingly recognize that the world goes on without their individual presence, they begin to seek alternative forms of self-justification in order to affirm their existence.

That alternative may lie in being oneself. It is an intangible and deeply personalized value. Individuals select values that others may not recognize, arrange them into their own narrative, and articulate why those values matter, ultimately defining the self. In this process, taste is the guiding standard. As the position of labor grows increasingly tenuous, taste becomes a means through which individuals construct themselves beyond imposed contexts.

Yet taste holds another potential. It separates the individual and the object from their context. Here, context refers to the societal and cultural frames that lead us to understand objects and experiences through predetermined meanings. Taste overlays the social uses and symbols attached to an object with the personal narrative that connects the individual to it, thereby severing the object from that context. In doing so, taste becomes the way individuals relate to the world and provides a driving force for life. 

Aura re-emerges from the distance between the individual and context. This distance is the gap created by the singular presence of the “here and now.” When one diverges from the originally intended meanings meant for everyone to consume along the same path and encounters the object through one’s own experience and language, the object acquires singularity. This is the moment when taste redeems the aura of the object.

At the same time, however, one must be wary of the contextualization of taste. This often begins with the pursuit of marginality, where a desire emerges to align oneself with a particular context, such as class, taste communities, or the logic of capital. Once absorbed into such a context, taste loses its aura. Contextualization turns it into a predictable trajectory that can be incorporated into the logic of capital. Contextualized taste sorts individuals into taste groups and objectifies them.

Ultimately, taste matters. Taste emerges not from what one chooses, but from the habit of continually asking why one chose it. When one can clearly articulate the reason for liking something, taste ceases to function as a language of classification and becomes a language of presence. It redeems aura and offers an experience through which one directly structures the way one lives.
