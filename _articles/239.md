As Seoul National University (SNU) celebrates its 80th anniversary in 2026, it is an appropriate moment not only to reflect on how far we have come but also to consider where we must go next. Founded in 1946, SNU has grown alongside modern Korea itself. The University's relocation to the Gwanak campus in 1975 marked the beginning of its transformation into a comprehensive research university. Over the ensuing decades, SNU has become the nation's leading institution of higher education, educating generations of leaders while advancing scholarship across virtually every academic discipline. Since the early 2000s, we have further strengthened our identity as a research-intensive university, pursuing excellence in both education and discovery.

These achievements deserve recognition. Yet anniversaries are valuable not because they celebrate the past alone, but because they invite honest reflection about the future. The world in which SNU was built is no longer the world in which it must compete. Although our academic excellence remains unquestioned within Korea, many of the institutional structures that continue to shape the University were established nearly half a century ago. During that same period, higher education has become fundamentally global. Universities now compete not only for domestic talent but also for the world's best students, scholars, and ideas. It is in this context that internationalization becomes more than an institutional objective. It becomes a defining challenge for SNU's next chapter.

SNU continues to attract Korea's brightest students at both the undergraduate and graduate levels. Our national reputation is unparalleled. Yet our international presence tells a different story. Compared with leading universities around the world—and even with several institutions within Seoul—the proportion of international students and faculty at SNU remains remarkably low. This reality is reflected in global assessments, where indicators related to internationalization consistently rank among the University's weakest areas. The issue, however, extends well beyond rankings. Internationalization is ultimately about the quality of our academic community. A university becomes globally influential not simply by sending its graduates abroad or publishing internationally, but by creating a campus where people from different countries, cultures, and intellectual traditions learn, teach, and discover together every day. Diversity is not an accessory to academic excellence; it is increasingly one of its essential conditions.

For many years, SNU's extraordinary success in attracting domestic talent may have reduced the urgency to compete globally. We have built an exceptional community within Korea. Our next task is to build an equally exceptional community that includes the world. Encouragingly, meaningful steps are now underway. The recent strengthening of the University's Office of Global Affairs reflects a growing institutional commitment to coordinated global engagement. Even more significant is the decision to establish the Division of Global Open Majors (tentative name), scheduled to welcome its first students in 2027. By admitting international students through an open-major system that allows them to explore multiple disciplines before choosing a field of study, the program represents not merely a new academic unit but a different philosophy of international education.

Preparing for this initiative has revealed an important truth. SNU is still far from being fully equipped to recruit, educate, and support international students at the scale required of a global university. In many respects, we must build new systems, policies, and cultures from the ground up. Yet this realization should be viewed not as a weakness but as an opportunity to undertake genuine institutional transformation.

Universities exist because there are students seeking knowledge. Everything else—faculty, staff, facilities, and governance—exists in service of that mission. If SNU succeeds in attracting a far more diverse international student body, many other dimensions of internationalization will follow naturally. International faculty will find a more vibrant academic community. Research collaborations will become broader and deeper. Korean students will develop global perspectives through everyday interactions with peers from around the world.

No university can pursue every possible reform at once. Strategic leadership requires focus. Rather than dispersing our limited resources across numerous incremental initiatives, we should invest where lasting transformation is most likely to occur. Building a truly international student community is one such investment. It has the potential to reshape every aspect of the University over time.

As SNU enters its ninth decade, our ambition should extend beyond remaining Korea's leading university. We should aspire to become one of the world's great universities—not only in research performance or global rankings, but in the diversity of our people, the openness of our culture, and the breadth of our intellectual community. That journey begins with a simple recognition: genuine internationalization is not a destination. It is the path by which SNU will define its future.
