*“Promise me…*  
*No matter what happens…*  
*Promise me that we’ll always be there for each other.”*

Released in 2020 by OMOCAT, *OMORI* is a psychological horror RPG that follows a group of friends—Sunny, Kel, Aubrey, Hero, Mari, and Basil—and the unfolding of an unspeakable tragedy that shattered them apart. Taking place four years after his sister Mari’s death, Sunny shuts himself indoors and creates a space of endless adventure but also an endless loop of avoidance inside his mind to conceal himself from the trauma. *OMORI* urges us to follow Sunny in exploring a surreal dream world as his titular alter ego Omori, alongside the real world, where he must face his past, mend broken friendships, and accept what has happened.  
As Omori, time never moves forward. It stays stagnant in the time before Mari’s death. On the surface, Omori’s world looks like a fantastical playdate with friends: bright and saturated with wonders. But brightness in the dream world is not always equivalent to happiness in reality. At its core, *OMORI* asks what it means to carry a traumatic event too heavy to hold alone. Beneath the colorful playfulness, *OMORI* constructs an architecture of trauma, of what happens when tragedy strikes and you are left with nothing but the horrors of when it happened.

**Places You Go When You Can’t Turn Back** 	  
*OMORI* presents Sunny's trauma through three layers, each a distinct world inside his mind: White Space as a place of rest and at length, emptiness; Headspace as the childlike images that mask the repressed trauma; and Black Space—located beneath Headspace—as the place that harbors Sunny’s fear and memories. While they differ vastly in texture and tone, each of them is an answer to the same question: *what do you do with something you cannot face?*  
	The player is first dropped into White Space where Omori lives, a never-ending loop of nothingness. Here, time appears motionless while the furniture is minimal to the point of emptiness. Although bedroom-adjacent in structure, the only items present are a laptop, a tissue box, a sketchbook, a cat, and a light bulb. The monochromatic and desaturated palette, coupled with a lack of shadows, flattens the game’s visuals. White Space is so stripped down of life that it almost feels serene. The flatness, however, harbors an eeriness beneath its stillness. White Space’s never-ending stretches of white presents an uncanny peacefulness that emanates from emptiness. For Sunny, the familiar comfort of the place keeps him calm, and the stability it brings helps prevent the trauma from resurfacing. Yet, White Space’s minimalistic design functions as a place of denial. In White Space, Sunny hides himself from reality.   
If White Space is an absence, Headspace is an excess. The place is oversaturated and overwhelmingly imaginative. In Headspace, the player moves through colorful environments that resemble a child’s dream, featuring hide-and-seek with your friends, a futuristic junkyard, and a sprout mole village. The Neighbor’s Room area—the first area we encounter after exiting White Space—sharply contrasts with the monochrome of the previous room. Entering the vibrant pink, yellow, and purple, Omori is immediately shoved into the noisiness of his best friends: Aubrey and Kel bicker, while Hero acts as the mediator. The colorful room features objects that resemble many of Sunny’s toys: cards and snacks scattered around, a toaster, a toy castle, and a gigantic yellow cat with eyes that follow you everywhere.   
Even outside of the Neighbor’s Room, areas in Headspace are full of fantastical elements such as an underground village that is home to the Sprout Moles (yes, sprouts that live like moles), or Sweetheart’s Castle, ruled by a tyrannical princess. Then there is the Last Resort, where you can drown yourself in casino games, party with ghosts, and fight Mr. Jawsum—a businessman shark—to rescue your friend from being tricked into fraudulent contracts. Everything in Headspace is playful to the point of absurdity. Built from Sunny’s imagination and boosted by his escapism, Headspace expands itself by piling absurdity upon absurdity until we lose ourselves in its excessiveness. *OMORI* deliberately protracts the Headspace section to make players reluctant to leave. By drawing us into its excessiveness, Headspace implicates us in Sunny's avoidance. Each detour is Sunny adding another barrier to shield himself from the pain. The more we immerse ourselves in Headspace’s adventures, the more effective his shield becomes.

**Places You Are Pushed Into**  
Although White Space and Headspace seem like polar opposites, they are actually two sides of the same coin. Just like how time is suspended in White Space, time stretches into an eternal childhood in Headspace. Many elements here are reassembled from Sunny’s past. The Neighbor’s Room is built upon the treehouse Sunny used to spend time in with his friends. Sweetheart is his toy doll, and Captain Spaceboy is a character from a franchise that he has seen in the real world.   
However, childhood memories, especially when translated into a dream world constructed to avoid reality, are never purely innocent. Just like how trauma eventually resurfaces, the veil is never fully drawn down in Headspace. Episodes such as the sudden breakdown of Sunny’s closest friend Basil or the appearance of Mari’s ghost occasionally disrupt the paradise. Like glitches, these intrusions expose the instability of Headspace, suggesting that it is not so much a memory as a construction of Sunny’s refusal to face the truth. Rather than moving on with life, Sunny creates a pseudo-reality that is always suspended in his childhood.  
Inevitably, everything violently comes to a head in Black Space. Positioned beneath Headspace, this area only becomes accessible after players collect the scattered keycaps across Headspace that spell out “Welcome to Black Space.” The floor in Basil’s house then collapses, and choosing to jump into the pit lets Omori enter this hidden layer of Sunny’s mind.   
Visually, Black Space is sparse and disorienting. The area consists of sections accessed through doors floating in a void, subverting players’ spatial awareness. Here, the structured Headspace breaks down and only the intrusive pain of Sunny’s past is left. Creatures and memories expelled by Headspace roam around each room. The main event of each area—Basil’s recurring deaths—illustrates Sunny’s attempts to protect himself from his repressed trauma. As someone directly tied to the incident surrounding Mari’s death and present at the scene, Basil occupies a fragile and unstable position within Sunny’s constructed world. If his breakdown in Headspace signals the flimsiness of that world, then his repeated deaths here are Sunny's attempts to suppress the part of his memory that threatens to expose the truth. Black Space feels like a fever dream, exposing how trauma cannot always be reshaped into an aesthetic narrative.

**The Space Between You and Me**  
*OMORI* extends its depiction of trauma beyond Sunny's internal collapse by incorporating his friends. Each character carries their emotions through their signature color. Aubrey is an angry red, Kel is a cheerful yellow, Hero is a sad but caring blue while Omori is black and unreadable. Their signature abilities in Headspace also mirror who they are in real life. Red, yellow, and blue extend from the dream world into Faraway Town—Sunny’s real world—and into the way each friend rebuilds themselves after Mari’s death. Aubrey shows aggressiveness through her appearance, dyeing her hair pink and joining the hooligans. Feeling abandoned by her friends and believing that Basil ruined their childhood photo album—the only artifact that archives their happiest memories together before the tragedy—she becomes his bully. By contrast, Kel and Hero, at least on the surface, seem to navigate the grief of Mari’s passing more easily. Kel is still happy and energetic, Hero still gentle and composed. However, *appearing* okay is not the same as *being* okay. Although the brothers appear stable, this does not mean they have forgotten Mari. If Aubrey’s red externalizes her pain, Kel and Hero’s colors suggest a quieter approach to grief.   
And then there are Sunny and Basil. Perhaps because they are the closest to Mari’s death, they become the most distant from everything else. Basil is haunted by a shadow he calls “something.” Sunny shut himself indoors both physically and emotionally. The toll of this withdrawal is reflected in his pale appearance, resembling the monochrome design of Omori. It is as if the line between Sunny and his constructed self—the line between fantasy and reality—has dissolved. During the first encounter with Aubrey after years of disconnect, Sunny swings a knife at her like how Omori does at the enemies in Headspace. The way Sunny directly applies Headspace’s fantastical logic into the real world shows how the period where Sunny “lived” as Omori has drastically disoriented himself.

**The Place You Return To**  
In *OMORI*, trauma is an architecture layered and reshaped from past memories. Trauma can be repressed, like in White Space. It can be structured into something safer as in Headspace, or can emerge uncontrollably as in Black Space. Presenting all these processes not in chronological order but in the way memory naturally unfolds when triggered, *OMORI* helps us understand what life might look like for a traumatized individual.  
As players, we understand that the characters care for each other deeply. However, their own wounds drive them apart, causing them to hurt one another, avoiding both confrontation and reconciliation. Sunny isolates himself and pretends everything is fine. Hero goes to college and continues to be the high-achieving figure others expect him to be. Aubrey alters herself to the point of unrecognition. Yet, her tough demeanor hides her loneliness from her broken family and friend group. In reality, Aubrey only wants the group to stay together and cling to each other after Mari’s death. She becomes the bully so that no one mistakes her for someone who could be hurt, as vulnerability requires trusting that people will stay.   
And perhaps that is what *OMORI* ultimately suggests. Although everyone grieves differently, grief is not meant to be carried alone. *OMORI*, at its core, is about what it means to do things together. The game answers that question with Basil’s photo album, the follow-up system to collaborate during battles, and the Release Energy attack, in which everyone cooperates to perform an all-out attack. And the fact that we can only trigger the Good Ending, where Sunny finally confronts his past and reconnects with his friends, by accepting Kel’s invitation to go outside suggests that the place you return to is none other than reality and your loved ones.   
The promise to always be there for each other is so fundamental that breaking it means breaking everything. What *OMORI* suggests is that trauma can both shape who we are and destroy our sense of self if carried alone. The bravest thing is not moving on and pretending nothing happened. *OMORI* tells us that it is turning back, trusting those around you, and carrying it together.
