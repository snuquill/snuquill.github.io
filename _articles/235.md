When people imagine climate activism, they usually picture the energy of the streets: protest signs, slogans, and collective voices demanding government action. While street activism remains the movement's bedrock, a new stage has recently come to the forefront: the courtroom. Globally, vulnerable communities least responsible for carbon emissions disproportionately bear the most disastrous consequences of global warming. Outmatched in political and economic leverage, they have taken their fight to the courts, reframing climate change not as a distant concern for nature, but as an immediate violation of human rights that demands legal accountability.

South Korea has emerged as a critical front in this global shift. In 2024, the Constitutional Court declared the government's emission targets unconstitutional, affirming that failing to protect future generations from climate harm violates fundamental rights. Hailed as Asia’s first major climate litigation victory, the decision forced the National Assembly to revise its Carbon Neutrality Framework Act by 2026\. This victory established a powerful precedent for state accountability, yet left one critical question unanswered: if the government must be held responsible for its policies, what responsibility should major corporate emitters bear for climate change?

That question now lies at the center of a civil lawsuit filed in August 2025 by five Korean farmers against majority state-owned utility giant Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) and its five subsidiaries. Supported by the legal team at Solutions for Our Climate (SFOC), the plaintiffs are seeking compensation for climate-related agricultural damages. According to an expert analysis cited by SFOC’s latest issue brief in August, KEPCO and its subsidiaries emitted approximately 2.56 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases between 2011 and 2023, accounting for roughly 31 percent of South Korea’s cumulative emissions and 0.5 percent of global emissions over the same period. This makes the case a major test of corporate climate accountability in Korea.

In an interview with *The SNU Quill*, attorney Kim Yeny of SFOC, lead counsel for the plaintiffs, describes climate litigation as a means to equip vulnerable groups with the legal and scientific tools to make the harms they face visible. Courts cannot deliver climate justice alone. But as political action continues to lag behind the scale of the crisis, litigation has become a last resort through which vulnerable citizens seek to protect their fundamental rights by translating climate harm into legal responsibility.

**The Rise of Climate Litigation: From Global Courts to Seoul**

Climate litigation has evolved as a transnational response to domestic legislative inaction. Following the 2015 Paris Agreement, lawsuits entered a new phase: plaintiffs increasingly framed climate inaction as a direct violation of human rights and constitutional duties. A milestone was achieved through the 2019 *Urgenda Foundation v. Netherlands* case, where Dutch courts, including the Supreme Court, held across successive appeals that the government must reduce emissions. This ruling established a legal duty to protect citizens from the dangers of climate change. Similar cases spreading across different continents cemented a global shift: climate change was now a matter of legal accountability.

The battlefront has since expanded to include corporate entities. For attorney Kim, a key comparative reference cited in the plaintiffs’ written submission for the lawsuit against KEPCO was *Lliuya v. RWE,* in which a Peruvian farmer sued the German energy giant RWE for a proportional share of flood-prevention costs based on the company’s contribution to global emissions. Although the plaintiff ultimately lost, the case remains significant because the court recognized, in principle, that major emitters can be held legally accountable for individual climate harm. It showed that such claims are no longer merely abstract, but part of an emerging global push to define corporate responsibility for climate change.

**South Korean Citizens’ Climate Battles in Court** 

South Korea’s 2024 Constitutional Court ruling emerged from a four-year legal battle led by 255 youth plaintiffs, including “Woodpecker,” an unborn child. Together, they argued that the state’s insufficient climate mitigation violated their fundamental rights, creating an untenable burden for future generations. The Court’s decision was a jurisprudential milestone, recognizing climate change as a constitutional issue and affirming that vulnerable generations are already suffering from state inaction.

According to Kim, the ruling established a critical precedent by grounding climate litigation in rights-based reasoning and validating scientific evidence as legally relevant. Yet, it left the issue of corporate accountability unresolved. While the state implements policy, corporations remain the primary drivers of emissions. The KEPCO lawsuit addresses this gap, challenging South Korean courts to confront the direct liability of major emitters.

***Ma et al. v. KEPCO: Making Corporate Climate Responsibility Justiciable*** 

In the KEPCO case, Kim serves as lead counsel within the plaintiffs’ legal team, helping design legal strategy and carry out advocacy. With the plaintiffs at the center of the litigation, the legal team’s role is to support them in translating their experiences and claims into legal arguments.

For Kim, climate litigation can be defined as litigation that seeks to “hold governments or corporations responsible for the climate crisis” and “bring about change” through the courts. What distinguishes it from ordinary environmental litigation is the unprecedented nature of climate change itself. “The climate crisis is a problem humanity is facing for the first time,” she said. It therefore requires lawyers to develop new approaches based on existing legal principles.

The KEPCO lawsuit attempts to apply those principles to corporate responsibility in Korea. Until now, Korean climate litigation has largely focused on government responsibility. This case asks whether major corporate emitters can be held liable for climate-related damages. Kim explained that efforts to hold corporations in check through lawsuits are growing globally, especially because they remain the largest contributors to emissions. By bringing Korea’s first damages claim against KEPCO and its subsidiaries, the lawsuit carries the meaning of Korea “joining that global trend.”

The central legal issue, Kim emphasized, is causation. Corporate defendants in climate litigations often argue that climate change is caused by countless actors emitting greenhouse gases over many decades. If climate change results from accumulated global emissions, they ask, why should one company be held responsible for a particular plaintiff’s harm? 

This is where attribution science becomes crucial. Kim explained that scientific methods have developed to the point where it is increasingly possible to assess how much a particular company’s emissions contributed to climate change, and how climate change contributed to specific forms of damage. In the KEPCO case, this means connecting several layers: the company’s cumulative emissions, those emissions’ cumulative contribution to the climate crisis, the cumulative impact of climate change on agriculture, and the specific damages suffered by the farmer plaintiffs.

Behind this technical legal question are farmers whose experiences make climate change concrete. Kim recalled the words of one apple farmer and activist plaintiff, Mr. Ma Yong-un, whose story stayed with her. When people hear about climate change, he told her, they often think of “polar bears somewhere far away in the Arctic.” But farmers in Korea are already suffering here, in the fields that feed people. For Kim, this moment captured why the lawsuit matters: climate change is not a distant abstraction, but a present harm affecting livelihoods, food production, and communities whose voices are often overlooked.

This is also why Kim sees climate litigation as more than a courtroom strategy. She described it as a way of giving vulnerable groups “weapons” of science and law. Through litigation, scientific evidence is gathered, legal arguments are developed, and the human consequences of a crisis often discussed in abstract numbers and images become harder to ignore.

**Beyond the Courtroom**

The question, then, is whether courts can actually change the behavior of governments or corporations. Kim’s answer was careful. Courts cannot replace legislatures or governments. They cannot design an entire energy transition, create all necessary regulations, or guarantee immediate implementation. The National Assembly’s failure to meet the February deadline for reforming the Carbon Neutrality Act serves as a stark reminder: even after a landmark court ruling, legislative inertia can stall the path to material change.

But Kim argued that litigation can still shape the conditions under which policy is made. By exposing corporate conduct and building public pressure, lawsuits force institutions to confront climate risks they would otherwise overlook.  

Recent developments in international courts may strengthen this trend. In 2025, the International Court of Justice issued a unanimous advisory opinion on climate change, emphasizing that states have legal obligations to protect the climate system and regulate private actors within their jurisdiction. For Kim, the opinion matters because it reframes the climate crisis from a political issue to a justiciable legal issue, while confirming that corporate contribution to harm is quantifiable despite its complexity. Furthermore, it establishes a heightened duty of care, shifting the inquiry beyond mere regulatory compliance to whether actors have taken all reasonable, good-faith measures to prevent climate harm.

Yet the courtroom is not a guaranteed path to justice; it is a battleground. There is a darker mirror to the legal turn. The same legal systems that allow vulnerable communities to challenge governments and corporations can also be used by powerful investors to challenge climate regulations, including through investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS) claims against states pursuing fossil fuel phase-outs, mining restrictions, or energy transitions. This does not make the use of litigations in the fight against climate change meaningless, but it complicates any simple faith in courts as automatically progressive spaces. 

Public attention, Kim emphasized, is not separate from the battles that take place behind the doors of courtrooms. “Judgments do not simply appear in isolation,” she explained. Courts reflect social currents and shared understandings. The more people talk about climate litigation and pay attention to the voices of vulnerable groups, the more those voices can shape the broader environment in which courts operate.

When I asked Kim whether public attention could truly make a difference in these arduous legal battles, her answer was immediate: it provides the strength needed to carry them forward. For young people grappling with climate anxiety, or the heavy feeling that individual actions are too small to stem a global crisis, these underdog legal battles offer a vital form of agency. Climate litigation is not a miracle cure, but it is a demonstration of resolve: a proof that even the most vulnerable groups will not resign to forces that may seem beyond their control. They are not merely waiting for change; they are actively creating an environment where fair, equitable accountability becomes possible. The burden is heavy, but no one has to carry it alone.
