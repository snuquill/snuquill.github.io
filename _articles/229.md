When a French PhD student in Computer Science and Engineering opened his usual email from SNU’s international office in early January, he thought he was looking at a typo. The subject, the formatting, the tone: everything was familiar, except the three letters at the top. OGA, where OIA used to be.

“Initially, I thought it was a typo,” he said. “Then I saw it was written multiple times. It didn’t seem to be \[one\].”

On January 1, 2026, SNU dissolved the Office of International Affairs (OIA) and replaced it with the Office of Global Affairs (OGA), reporting to the Executive Vice President for Planning and Communication and split into two divisions: International Cooperation and International Education. On paper, the rebrand is an upgrade, a promotion from *bonbu*, a sub-unit, to *cheo*, a full office with higher administrative standing. In practice, none of the students interviewed had been officially notified. Most found out the way the French PhD student had: through an email, wondering if it was a mistake. This article asks what has actually changed since January, and what has not.

**A rebrand with pressure behind it**

The rebrand did not come out of nowhere. One source of pressure was the university ranking. In the November 2025 QS World University Rankings: Asia, SNU placed 17th, behind Yonsei (11th), Korea University (12th), and Sungkyunkwan (16th). Five months earlier, SNU had slid seven places in the QS World University Rankings to 38th. Among the indicators QS clusters as “Global Engagement”—international research network and exchange agreements, inbound and outbound student flows—SNU has historically scored well below its domestic peers, widely viewed to have dragged down those headline numbers.

Another factor was enrollment. International students make up roughly 4% of SNU’s student body, about 1,795 as of October 2024; at Yonsei and Korea University, the number nears 16%. South Korea’s “Study Korea 300K” plan reached its 2027 target two years early, with 305,329 foreign students nationwide as of August 2025\. SNU’s numbers amount to roughly 0.6% of that total, a thin slice for the country’s flagship university.

Leadership moved to frame what the new office signified. On January 2, President Ryu Hong Lim’s New Year’s address called for stronger internationalization across “teaching, research, social contribution and administration.” A week later, inaugural Vice President Kim Taekyoon presented the “3G Initiatives”: Global Network, Global Leadership, Global Engagement, and announced a first-year International Student and Scholar Services (ISSS) center to centralize support for international students and faculty.

**Why the change matters**

To understand what the upgrade from *bonbu* to *cheo* actually means, we have to start with a feature of SNU few people notice. Unlike most institutions, where headquarters issue directives and colleges execute them, SNU has historically operated more like a federation: each college with its own power, its own systems, its own way of treating international students and faculty.

The OIA was a sub-unit with limited authority under a research vice presidency. The OGA, under the Executive Vice President for Planning and Communication, is now the link between headquarters and all of SNU’s colleges on any international matter. Its restructuring documentation states the goal is to “eliminate departmental silos and provide the administrative leverage necessary to manage integrated international strategies at the headquarters level.” OGA staff member Lee Hyeokju emphasized: “OGA now centralizes, integrates and manages them. The aim is to provide equal services.”

**Four months in: what changed, what didn’t**

SISA, the SNU International Student Association, has felt the structural shift sharply. Under the OIA, one person advised both SISA and the office’s working-student team, many of whom are SISA members. Under the OGA, that role has been split in two.

“Communication has had to have a little bit more deliberate effort, deliberate action from our side,” SISA’s Administrative Member and Treasurer said. The new office launched its first formal review of SISA and requested a full budget report. The power imbalance is now explicit, “but not in a bad way. They have the final say, and we work under them.” Those new structures, she said, are what make it workable.

SISA’s President, a Mozambican undergraduate in Industrial Engineering, sees the same shift through an anticipatory lens. Where the OIA largely left SISA to run the community, the OGA now expects concrete output. “We’re currently in a phase where we have to prove that we’re actually useful,” she noted.

That shift, however, has not been matched by resources. SISA’s budget has decreased for years even as international enrollment has grown, the President said. The OGA’s own documents say its budget is “concentrated on expanding scholarships for global talent, building digital administrative infrastructure, and enhancing settlement services,” none of which funds the student-led community work that, the President said, the OGA “would have to do” otherwise.

The friction has been most visible in front-line staff rotations, with a new team learning the job from scratch. A Japanese master’s student in Korean Language Education, who completed her bachelor’s at SNU, faced that mismatch trying to change her D-2 visa status after a department change. “The manager in charge of visa changes was new, and they weren’t sure how to process the visa for new students,” she said. She exchanged more than ten emails without a consistent answer. “If the previous staff makes a Q\&A list of ‘I sorted out this situation with this kind of answer,’ it would be much better,” she said.

In an incident reported to *The SNU Quill*, similar frictions in scholarship processing became apparent. A student asked why an overdue housing payment had not arrived; the student worker was told to relay only that she should wait, with no timeline. The student left in tears. “\[My superior\] keeps saying, ‘I’m changing the systems,’” the student worker said. “That’s why I can’t tell the students to come again or just wait for the email.”

Outside the building, change is practically nonexistent. The OGA can still be reached at i-office@snu.ac.kr, the “i” for International, a remnant from an office that no longer officially exists. Step outside Building \#152 and the signage still reads “International Center.” The French PhD student finds these details telling. “If you want to rebrand the name, you need to be careful. Everywhere the name appears needs to be changed,” he said. “At least before, it was clear: International Building, Office of International Affairs, international office email. It wasn’t confusing.”

The OGA’s notice board tells the same story of nonexistent change. Between January and mid-April, announcements have been almost entirely routine: shuttle schedules, campus events, and recruitment for the SNU In The World Program. The ISSS center, the OGA’s flagship first-year initiative, has been announced but not opened. It exists in posters and press releases, not in office space that students can walk into.

The gap between SISA and the rest of SNU’s student governance also remains untouched. Unlike the general student council, SISA has no direct channel to the administrative office; its proposals on housing, language, and dormitory access continue to flow through the OGA. “If we have the strength to carry a petition to the administrative office of SNU, that would be great,” SISA’s President said. “But I don’t think SISA has the \[administrative\] strength to do that.” On that question, the rebrand has changed nothing. The issues international students bring to SISA—feeling left out of department activities, visa, language barriers—are, in the Treasurer’s words, “kind of the same as it is every semester.” Yonsei opened permanent rolling transfer admissions in 2025 and cultivates feedback from its international cohort; Korea University continues to expand its intake. At SNU, the most concrete public-facing product of the first OGA semester is more frequent email correspondence and a weekly ISSS newsletter.

**The office responds**

Lee did not dispute that. The slow pace, he claims, is the point: “It’s only been four months since OGA started. Less than 120 days. So, I can’t say in detail yet.”

He pushed back on the competitive framing. “It is not an attempt to win a competition. It’s about upgrading the essence of Seoul National University a bit more. If you change something just to beat someone, once you win, the goal disappears.”

On the choice of name: “International is kind of country-based. But global is a more comprehensive concept. We want to achieve internationalization in a broader framework. That is OGA’s goal.”

Lee’s five-year horizon expands the OGA’s mandate beyond exchange students, language trainees and MOUs with foreign universities, to a visiting professor program, support for foreign professors’ families, joint research, and central authority over English-language instruction.

He was candid about vision versus capacity. “Right now, there are only four people \[working towards the five-year plan\]. Four people is too few to manage the whole campus.” The office has employed four to five new staff since January, mostly on the education side. The OGA’s limits are also clear: the Global Talent College, opening in 2027, is designed at the headquarters and college-dean level; visa rules belong to Korea; English content on department websites depends on each college.

For Lee, the OGA’s job is to make SNU a place international students want to stay. “\[The OGA’s\] goal is to help the community settle in Korea in every aspect of life and study. Not just visa services, but helping them feel familiar and safe in the space called this university so they think, ‘I like this school. It’s good. I should stay here longer. Even more, I should settle in Korea.’”

**A new chapter, or a new sign?**

The students and staff interviewed were not hostile to the OGA. Several were hopeful. SISA’s Treasurer pointed to the linguistic promotion from *bonbu* to *cheo* as a signal she expects to see reflected in the office’s activities. “I don’t think they just did it for fun,” she said. SISA’s President agreed the change is “probably a start to something,” and a Malaysian undergraduate working student called it “a new chapter.”

What students are looking for is operational: faster email replies; more classes taught in English; a Q\&A for common questions; an OGA presence on Yeongeon campus, where Medicine, Nursing and Dentistry sit outside the international support at Gwanak. None of these demands are radical. All of them would have tangible effects.

“In the next five years, there’s a lot to be expected,” SISA’s President emphasized. “I believe that even international faculty will probably hold a much greater power than it does right now.” Whether that expectation is met depends on the OGA’s ability to make SNU more international in ways most visible to the 4% that makes up the international student body in SNU, especially now that they have newfound authority to directly manage the colleges. 

Four and a half months in, what the 4% can see is a sign that has not caught up to itself. The rest of the change, Lee claims, is upstairs. Whether it comes downstairs next year will determine if the *cheo* is a promotion that means something, or a face that does not lead to a body.
