It’s 2025. As you begin your daily shift of doomscrolling on your phone, you watch through your screen as a man kneels in a field of gray rubble, scrambling in shock through the dust for his four children buried beneath a city flattened to the ground. Scroll again, and children’s cries overlap in the background as a boy, standing amidst the chaotic aftermath of an airstrike on civilian buildings, looks into the camera and screams: “Where is the world? Oh God, where is the world?” By the pure chance of your algorithm, you, an ordinary citizen, have become an involuntary, yet undeniable witness to what former UNRWA spokesman Chris Gunness has called the “first livestreamed genocide” in human history.
 
Far from being a distant regional tragedy, Gaza stands at the center of what legal scholars warn is an increasingly widespread erosion of fragile global norms. This has ominous implications for the rule-based international order that has aimed to keep state conduct in check. If a nation’s ethnic cleansing of an entire civilian population and the violent seizure of their occupied land becomes diplomatically defensible, what precedent does that set for international politics in the 21st century? If deliberate disregard of international law incurs no economic or diplomatic cost, what prevents nations from acting without restraint? And perhaps more urgently: do these rules apply, as they claim to, to every single human being on this planet—or only to those whom powerful states decide are worthy of protection?
 
Neither the reader nor I will ever meet Ahmed, the little Palestinian farmer boy in Gaza, and his black cat named Simba. His nation has yet to even be recognized as a state by mine. Yet the same global norms obliging states to uphold international humanitarian law are the railings safeguarding all of us from an anarchic world governed by hard power alone. With the risk of losing these norms comes a wave of global backlash: an expanding grassroots boycott and solidarity movement, mounting investigations by NGOs and IGOs, division between Western powers, as well as a historic surge in the recognition of the state of Palestine. As Gaza now stands as one of the last frontlines upholding the legitimacy of international law and all that it stands for, South Korea cannot afford to look away.
 
**Crimes against Humanity: Who Counts as Humanity?**
 
Israel has consistently justified its military operations in Gaza by invoking its legal right to self-defense (*jus ad bellum*) against the armed militia Hamas in response to the horrific October 7 attacks. Its response found initial support, especially in the West. However, the excessive force used by the Israeli Defense Forces has since been widely criticized, as they have been accused of going beyond the principle of proportionality—a legal duty which, if not upheld, constitutes a state’s actions as war crimes under international law.
 
In fact, Israel’s zeal for using excessive force upon the Palestinian population in Gaza has been deemed so blatantly disproportionate as to suggest that the targeting of innocent civilians, not Hamas, has been the true goal of its military operations. This is an allegation bolstered by leading genocide scholars as well as the nation’s longstanding history of Zionism and forced expulsion of indigenous Palestinians since 1948. In 2023, accusations were brought by South Africa and 14 supporting countries (mostly from post-colonial and Global South territories) against Israel before the International Court of Justice (ICJ). Under the 1948 Genocide Convention, 153 signatory states (including South Korea) are legally obliged to prevent and punish the crime put forward. However, Western powers, including the US, rejected the allegations, while Israel ignored the Court’s provisional measures to let aid pass into the Gaza Strip. To many in the Global South, the toleration of human suffering in Gaza epitomizes the double standards of the Western‑led, rule‑based global order.
 
Amidst continued escalation in Gaza, on November 21, 2024, the International Criminal Court (ICC) in The Hague issued its most controversial warrants to date against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant, and Hamas military chief Mohammed Deif for war crimes and crimes against humanity. Since commencing operations in 2002, ICC arrest warrants have been overwhelmingly concentrated on individuals from the African continent. This has fueled accusations that the organization delivers selective justice and operates as a neo-colonial instrument of Western dominance. Therefore, the warrants against Israel hold crucial significance: for the first time, leaders of a Western-aligned democracy have been indicted by the court for war crimes and crimes against humanity. Beyond symbolism, the warrants now constrain Netanyahu’s travel, as any transit through Rome Statute parties risks arrest.
 
The ICC now faces a legitimacy crisis, as prominent Western states have openly challenged its authority. Experts such as Yale Law professor Oona Hathaway warn that without a new multilateral coalition of like-minded states cooperating to uphold its principles, the international rule-based legal order is on the brink of collapse.
 
**Why Cynicism is The Bane of International Law**

The core norms of contemporary international law were not granted by nature, but forged in response to the catastrophic atrocities of the two World Wars and the Holocaust. Dismissing these bloodstained norms today actively brings about their deterioration. International law lives or dies with *opinio juris*—the belief that a practice is legally required—which is fundamentally shaped by the attitudes and perceptions held by states.

The claim that morality has no place in foreign policy is refuted by the fact that public opinion, especially in democracies, shapes how a state’s national interest is defined. Citizens around the world have reacted with outrage to the realization that their governments and tax money are contributing to atrocities now being livestreamed by their victims in the Gaza Strip. Coordinated public protests and boycotts against Israel have spread across art, culture, and education, with global pro-Palestine protests soaring since 2023. Under rising pressure, several Western countries have imposed sanctions and embargoes on Israel and moved towards recognizing Palestine as a state.

Public opinion in South Korea has followed a similar trend, with recent polls indicating that 59% view Israel’s military actions in Gaza as excessive. Pro-Palestinian college student movements such as *SNU Soobak*, working with the *People in Solidarity with Palestine*, have called for South Korea to end its complicity in Israel’s continued military offensives in Gaza and the wider Middle East.

This growing mobilization suggests that a global movement of citizens advocating for a decolonial, rule-based international order, exemplified by the struggle for Palestinian liberation, could reach a “tipping point.” In Finnemore and Sikkink’s theory of the life cycle of norms, such a moment marks the transition towards the “norm cascade stage.” In this stage, newly consolidated norms rapidly diffuse as states adopt them under converging international expectations and domestic pressures. Alternatively, if cynicism prevails and states continue to treat international law as expendable whenever it proves inconvenient, this moment may not usher in a cascade of stronger norms but their gradual death. Repeated violations without consequence teach both governments and citizens that the rules were never binding.

**South Korea at a Crossroads: A Question of National Identity**

In October 2025, a UN report titled *Gaza Genocide: a collective crime* listed South Korea among complicit nations for supplying Israel with components for F-35 fighter jets used in airstrikes on the Gaza Strip. The Korea National Oil Corporation has pursued oil and gas exploration rights off the Gaza coast—resources denied to Palestinians and exploited by Israel. The recent ADEX defense exhibition hosted Israeli arms manufacturers, including Rafael, whose promotional video showed its drone killing an unarmed Palestinian civilian. South Korea also remains one of only five G20 nations that have not recognized Palestine as a state—a stance likely shaped by Washington’s unilateral support for Israel.

Over 70% of South Korea’s oil imports come from the Middle East, making its energy security deeply tied to regional stability. Its interest, therefore, does not lie in the opportunistic exploitation of human suffering and resources in Gaza, but in a positive peace. Defined by Johan Galtung, positive peace is distinguishable from the mere absence of active war and violence. It is a peace grounded in attitudes, institutions, and structures sustaining a durable condition of peace, in which armed resistance movements like Hamas would have no reason to exist. Such a peace can only exist with the liberation of the Palestinian people under a state that grants them self-determination and freedom from occupation.

As Alexander Wendt famously argued, “anarchy is what states make of it.” The international system is not doomed to be a lawless struggle for survival, but becomes one when states choose power and impunity over restraint and cooperation. As a middle power in an increasingly interdependent world and a rule-of-law democracy, South Korea must take an active stand in the global movement for international justice. Will Korea remain at the mercy of hegemonies or assume responsibility as a norm‑enforcing middle power?
