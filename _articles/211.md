On December 3, 2024, former president Yoon Sukyeol incited a failed military coup. Not only did the botched attempt lead to the ousting of the conservative Yoon administration, but it also intensified division across political lines. Although the National Assembly voted in favor of Yoon’s impeachment, many of his supporters believed the procedure was unfair. At the frontlines of the battle were conservative Christians. The National Movement Headquarters and the Save Korea Movement (led by Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon of Sarang Jeil Church and Pastor Son Hyun-bo of Segyero Church, respectively) were two of the major organized protests pressuring the Constitutional Court to reject the former president’s impeachment. These demonstrations took place in various locations such as Gwanghwamun Square and Yeouido from December 2024 to April 2025, when the court found Yoon’s impeachment legal. Though substantially weakened, such movements continue today. Critics have branded them “the extreme right.”

In a country home to one of the world’s largest churches, and where more than 30% of the population identifies as Christian, the question looms: how did Korean Christianity become entangled with politics, and what does this mean for both church and state?

**The Church Aligns with the Right**

The alignment of the Church with the conservative right in South Korea can be traced back to the birth of the republic. The key factor was anti-communism. The peninsula’s Christian community was most numerous in the Northwest region. After a socialist government was set up in the North, many headed south to flee persecution. These groups formed the core of the new republic’s Christian population and instilled a strong fear against Marxist ideology in the nation. Combined with the needs of the US and then-president Rhee Syngman (who was Christian himself) to promote anti-communism in the country, it was a natural consequence that the Christian community became a powerful player in the political scene.

The dictatorships of Park Chung Hee and Chun Doo-hwan strengthened the alliance. In exchange for access to church services within the military and permission for large-scale Christian events, the Church provided support for the authoritarian regimes. For example, 242 pastors from major denominations, including Han Kyungjik of Youngnak Church and Jo Yong-gi of Yoido Gospel Church, released a statement supporting Park’s third-term amendment. Pastor Kim Joon Gon established Campus Crusade for Christ, a youth ministry that became a platform for promoting anti-communist ideology and loyalty to the government. Park actively supported the organization to influence the ideology of young adults. Government-level support, combined with the urban population boom of the 1980s, led to the rise of megachurches with tens of thousands of registered members. These giants accumulated wealth and power through special benefits, such as tax exemptions for religious assets and regulatory leniency on church buildings.

**The Church Becomes a Political Power**

The early 2000s’ Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun administrations marked a turning point. Progressive policies conflicted with megachurch interests: peace talks with North Korea contradicted anti-communist ideology; anti-discrimination reforms clashed with fundamentalist views on LGBTQ+ rights; stricter property and private school regulations threatened accumulated privileges. Conservative churches responded by openly organizing political protests, cementing their transformation into partisan actors.

There are numerous examples of right-wing Christian activism throughout the 2010s and 2020s. These groups sought to protect former president Park Geun-hye from impeachment, protested against COVID-19 regulations in the name of freedom of worship, and organized rallies against immigrants and other religions, particularly Islam. The Christian right has been especially vocal in voicing hostility towards the LGBTQ+ community; it has repeatedly pressured politicians to cancel queer parades and barred LGBTQ+ people from becoming pastors. In 2024, approximately 1.1 million Christians gathered in Gwanghwamun Square for a “United Worship Against Evil Laws,” referring to anti-discrimination laws that advocate equal rights for the LGBTQ+ community.

Today, one of the most extreme voices of the Christian right is Jeon Kwang-hoon. Since 2019, he has been leading rallies against progressive administrations (then Moon Jae-in, and now Lee Jae Myung) and in support of the conservative Yoon government. He accuses the left-wing politicians of being spies of communist North Korea, and preaches that one should follow his ideas to reach salvation. He has also led protests against former president Yoon’s impeachment and has been accused of inciting the Seoul Western District Court riot.

Along with Jeon, Pastor Son Hyun-bo of Segyero Church led the Save Korea protests, also calling for the Constitutional Court to reject Yoon’s impeachment. He has since been imprisoned due to his violation of election laws, after publicly endorsing the conservative candidate Kim Moon-soo during the 21st Korean presidential elections.

Pastor Kim Jang-hwan of Suwon Central Baptist Church is another name that has been associated with collusion with the government. He has allegedly pulled strings to help military officials evade responsibility for the death of Corporal Chae, who died during a military operation as a result of negligence by his superiors.

**The Church Needs Reform**

The Church’s alignment with politics is detrimental to public trust. A 2025 Christian Institute for Justice and Development survey found that 61.8% of respondents reported declining trust in Christianity following anti-impeachment rallies.

In addition, religious political pressure undermines democratic pluralism. When churches wield institutional power to influence legislation and mobilize voters, they compromise the principle of neutral democracy that should serve all citizens. As the Korean constitution clearly states, “No state religion shall be recognized, and religion and state shall be separated.”

Not all conservative Christians embrace extremism; studies show 21.8% of Christians self-identify as “extreme-right,” a figure comparable to the general population’s 21%. Yet the lukewarm institutional responses blur these lines between extremism and complacency. Many churches neither actively condemn nor clearly distance themselves from extremist actions, thereby creating implicit endorsement. For example, after the arrest of Son Hyun-bo, his denomination (Kosin) convened in a General Assembly and released a statement arguing that his arrest was “political oppression.” 

Progressive Christian voices do exist. The National Council of Churches in Korea (NCCK), Christian Ethics Movement of Korea (CEMK), and the Protest2002 Alliance have urged the Kosin denomination to discipline Son and have called for the Christian Council of Korea (CCK) to label Jeon as a heretic. However, they are still a relative minority in South Korea’s Christian landscape, and their demands have largely gone unanswered.

**Give to Caesar what is Caesar’s**

It would be unjust to say that all churches in South Korea are extreme-right in nature. Quite the contrary—churches have historically led many of the societal advances that have shaped Korean society. Christians actively participated in the independence movement under Japanese colonial rule; 16 of the 33 national leaders who drafted the 3.1 Declaration of Independence were Christians. Churches also played a critical role in the path to democracy, supporting the 5.18 Movement and the 1987 June Protests. 

Secularism—the separation of church and state—is a longstanding tradition rooted even in biblical teaching: “Give to Caesar what is Caesar’s, and to God what is God’s” (Mark 12:17). The accumulation of wealth and power within churches, however, often leads to further collusion with the government. This issue is compounded by the fact that in many Korean churches, power is largely concentrated in the hands of pastors, a natural consequence of many megachurches being founded by charismatic leaders. Moreover, many of these churches uphold hereditary succession practices in an attempt to preserve personal influence and wealth. 

Thus, a thorough reorganization of power within the Church would serve as a starting point for the reform of megachurches. Critics suggest breaking large churches down into smaller ones, establishing congregational governance systems, and strengthening oversight by general assemblies. Secularism, in this sense, grants churches the freedom to act solely according to Christian values, thereby curtailing the influence of material ambition.

**Towards a Public Religion**

The Church should participate in a “public religion,” not a political one. The term, coined by American sociologist Robert Wuthnow, distinguishes between providing an ethical norm through democratic processes and advocating for a specific political party. Instead of turning a blind eye to the injustices in society while attacking those who are different, the Church should prioritize advocating for the rights of the underrepresented. That would even mean embracing the very communities the megachurches often condemn.

The persistence of Christian extremism in South Korea represents a crisis, but also an opportunity for the Church. Its current loss of credibility and reputation due to its entanglement with political powers is a real problem, but it can also catalyze reform.

Change won’t be easy, as it requires the Church to admit its shortcomings, potentially losing its power and members who prioritize political alignment over religious conscience. But the choice is clear: conservative Korean churches can continually support fundamentalist and extremist actions, or they can redirect themselves to contribute to positive social change.
