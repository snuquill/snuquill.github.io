Confession time: I did not always want to become a journalist.

I initially pursued political science and international relations with hopes of becoming a diplomat. Being in far-flung places, witnessing history firsthand, and creating palpable change were what made the vocation so enticing.

Reality, however, hit hard going into my sophomore year of college. The more I studied, and the more diplomat seonbaes I met, the clearer it became that diplomacy was—at least at the start—just another desk job.

I realized I had been naive. Model United Nations delegates had far more authority than junior officials entering the foreign ministry. Creating tangible change would have to wait decades, as report after report piled up on a desk.

Journalism only rose as an alternative after I fled to the military.

Under extreme surveillance and tight information control, the only connection the roughly 40 cadets in my platoon had to the outside world was a small Samsung television perpetually tuned to YTN, Korea’s largest news channel.

The few hours of television we were allowed on weekends were precious. This was before phones were permitted. It was there that I realized journalism was the one profession that allowed me to be in different places, witness history as it unfolded, and create impact through agenda-setting. 

Upon discharge, I began paving my path toward a career in reportage—and, admittedly, a fair amount of alcoholism.

**A Declining Industry**

I started my career at *The Korea Herald*, my current company, after securing an internship straight out of the military. Around the same time, I joined *The SNU Quill* as an SNU Society writer.

I still remember the first article I wrote. I had enthusiastically submitted what I thought was an eloquent piece, filled with flowery language and flamboyant vocabulary—only to see it entirely dissected, rewritten, and reduced to something unrecognizable. I quickly realized how much I still had to learn.

This was before the rise of ChatGPT and generative AI. Even then, pessimism about journalism’s future was omnipresent.

“It’s a declining industry,” and “you should rethink your career,” were phrases I heard from both inside and outside the field.

My parents, whose lifelong motto had been “let him do what he wants,” could not understand why I had chosen print over newer media. Still, I persisted. I loved writing. I loved the immediate gratification of meeting people who had read my work. Yet beneath that satisfaction lingered a quiet anxiety: the persistent what if.

**The Dawn of AI**

The arrival of ChatGPT reshaped the writing industry almost overnight, and sparked deep divisions. By then, I had launched my freelance career, writing for international publications such as the *South China Morning Post* and *The New York Times*.

Newsrooms scrambled to respond. Some outlets, including *The New York Times*, encouraged limited use of generative AI. Others, like *The Straits Times*, imposed outright bans. Still most offered no guidance at all.

Meanwhile, AI’s writing ability improved by the second. Discussions about journalism’s future dominated both newsrooms and classrooms. As a college senior and freelance journalist, I was fortunate to witness, and participate in, both.

**Yes to Decline, No to Disappearance**

First, here is the uncomfortable truth: reporters will be replaced by AI—but only the mediocre ones.

Yet, after speaking with experts and witnessing AI’s impact firsthand, I came to a clear conclusion: AI does not pose an immediate existential threat to journalism.

That confidence rests on two pillars AI cannot replace: the human demand for credible information, and the deeply human nature of reporting.

AI excels at analyzing writing patterns and producing stylized text. It already outperforms humans in writing market earnings reports, sports recaps, and financial summaries. But many outside the industry mistakenly believe journalism begins and ends with writing.

In reality, writing is only the tip of the iceberg.

From personal experience, more than 70 percent of my work is spent fact-checking, cultivating sources, probing allegations, attending events, strategizing information access, and speaking with people who shape policy and power. These are things AI cannot do better than humans.

AI may write faster. But it cannot knock on doors, earn trust, or sit in a courtroom and decide what matters.

Moreover, as information becomes more accessible through platforms like YouTube, Naver, and AI-powered search, verified and trustworthy accounts have grown ironically scarcer. In that environment, journalism’s value does not diminish. It sharpens.

Journalism may be shrinking, fragmenting, and redefining itself. But it is not disappearing. As long as power exists, so will the need for people willing to question it. As long as information circulates freely, someone must verify it.

I did not become a journalist because it was safe, lucrative, or future-proof. I became one because it remains necessary.

And for students wondering whether journalism still has a place in an age of AI, my answer is simple: if you are curious, stubborn, and human enough, there is still room for you.
