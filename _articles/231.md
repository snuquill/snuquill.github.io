It was past ten on a Wednesday when my phone lit up. Someone from the Business School football club had texted the group chat: anyone willing to join an after-party at SNU Station was welcome. I was already back in my room in Daehak-dong, halfway through a bag of chips. I put on a jacket and left. The whole process took maybe forty seconds.

I did not think much of it at the time. But later that night, walking back at 2 a.m. through Nokdu-geori, past the convenience store that never closes and the skewer stand that somehow always has a line, it occurred to me that not everyone in that group chat could make the same call. Some of them had seen the message and done a different kind of math: the last train from SNU Station, the transfer, the walk home, and the morning class waiting the next day. The answer, for them, was no.

At SNU, housing is often treated as a logistical problem, something to sort out before the semester begins, then set aside. But it does not stay settled. It follows students into every late-night group chat, every gap between classes that is too short to go home and too long to use well, every meeting that runs past the last train. Where you live quietly shapes what version of SNU you get to experience. And those versions are not the same.

## **What It Actually Costs**

A standard one-room near Nokdu-geori or Daehak-dong usually requires a deposit of KRW 3,000,000 to 10,000,000 upfront, with monthly rent often around KRW 500,000 before utilities. It is not cheap, and that is still the relatively student-friendly end of the neighborhood. Shared housing can bring the monthly cost down, but it comes with its own frictions: finding a compatible roommate, adjusting to shared schedules, and giving up privacy.

On paper, the university dormitory is the most rational option. It is cheaper than private rent, close to campus, and removes the daily commute almost entirely. The problem is supply. Dorm beds fall short of demand, and selection is weighted toward students from outside Seoul. Students who miss the lottery often end up in the private rental market anyway, sometimes under time pressure and with limited options. As one student put it, “I used to commute for more than four hours a day just because my registered address was in Seoul.”

Commuting from home eliminates rent, which is a serious financial advantage. But it replaces that cost with something less visible: time. A student commuting from Incheon, Bundang, or the northern edge of Seoul can easily spend three to four hours in transit each day. Across a sixteen-week semester, that adds up to well over two hundred hours, more than eight full days, spent simply getting to and from campus. Unlike rent, this does not appear in a bank statement, but it’s still significant.

## **The Social Arithmetic of the Last Train**

The last train does not negotiate. Once you miss it, your options are a taxi that costs more than a day’s meals, or staying somewhere you had not planned to stay. Many commuting students, especially in their first year, develop something like a parallel schedule: the official schedule everyone sees, and the private one always tracking departure times underneath it.

The effect of this issue on social life is gradual and hard to measure, but very real. Much of SNU’s club culture happens after evening lectures, between seven and midnight, when things actually begin to unfold: after-parties after performances, spontaneous meetups, senior-junior dinners at pubs near SNU Station, and the casual conversations where career advice gets passed across a table. Commuting students are not excluded by rule. They are excluded by time.

“I stopped going to club meetings as much in my second semester,” Park, a senior, said. Once that pattern starts, it often reinforces itself. The less often you show up, the less central you become. The less central you feel, the harder it is to justify another long ride home for one more meeting.

## **When the Trade-Off Stops Being Abstract**

A freshman on the Business School football team, commuting from Nowon, faced that situation this semester. Morning practice starts at 6 a.m. The subway does not run early enough to get him there on time. So he, along with a few other commuting teammates, began sleeping in the department lounge the nights before training. Not once or twice, but regularly across several weeks. It solved the transportation problem, but introduced a different one: sleeping on a couch before a full day of classes is not actual rest.

This is an extreme case, but it points to something common. Commuting students often absorb costs in sleep, money, and comfort that are invisible to everyone else. The decision to stay late for a team commitment does not end when the commitment ends. It has a tail: getting home, paying for a taxi, or giving up on going home at all.

Group projects, already one of the more stressful parts of business school life, also change when some members have hard departure times. The student who needs to leave by ten cannot always be the one who stays to polish the presentation. Over a semester, reputations form not only from ability or effort, but from availability. It just accumulates.

## **Proximity as a Quiet Advantage**

Students who live near campus do not necessarily work harder or manage their lives better;  they just face less friction.

A ninety-minute gap between classes looks completely different depending on where you live. From Daehak-dong, it is enough time to go home, eat properly, rest, and come back. From Incheon or Bundang, it is a gap too wide to ignore and too short to use comfortably. You stay on campus, half-working and half-waiting, until the next class begins.

The same logic applies to networking. A senior who grabs coffee with a junior after a club meeting, a professor who continues discussions with students who stick around after class, a recruiter who remembers the face of someone who showed up twice to a career fair—these moments are not equally accessible to everyone.

I have felt this myself. Some of the most useful conversations I have had at SNU happened only because I could stay a little longer: an after-party that turned into advice from a senior, a walk from Building 58 down to the bus stop where someone mentioned an opportunity, a meeting that continued after the official part was over. Commuting students can and do build networks, but it takes more deliberate effort to recreate what proximity provides by coincidence.

## **A Private Problem with Structural Roots**

None of this is the result of individual failure. The students commuting from the outskirts are not less committed. The freshman sleeping in the department lounge is not simply bad at time management. They are navigating a system that does not offer equally accessible options.

SNU provides dormitories, but not at a scale that meets demand. The private rental market around Gwanak-gu keeps proximity expensive for students without family support, savings, or part-time income. Beyond the housing office’s basic listings, there is also limited institutional support for helping students navigate the trade-offs they are being asked to make.

One recent example of this tension is SNU’s Living and Learning program, or LnL. On paper, LnL treats dormitory life as more than a place to sleep: it is also meant to be a space for mentoring, community-building, and education. In that sense, it supports the argument this article is making: where students live matters. But for students who cannot get a dorm room in the first place, that vision can feel distant. If limited dormitory space is being used to create curated residential programs while many commuting students still struggle to secure basic housing near campus, the uncomfortable question is whether SNU is improving residential life for those already inside the system, or expanding access for those left outside it.

Even for students who do make it into the dormitories, the experience is not always the perfect solution it appears to be from the outside. Dorm life may solve distance, but it can come with aging facilities, shared spaces, strict rules, limited privacy, and a living environment that feels more functional than comfortable. Expanding dorm access, then, is not only about increasing the number of beds. It is also about whether the university can provide housing that genuinely supports students’ daily lives.

For most students, housing remains a problem to solve personally each year before the semester starts, even as the university continues to experiment with what dormitory life could become.

## **No Clean Answer, But a Question Worth Asking**

There is no objectively correct place to live as an SNU student. The answer depends on finances, family situation, commute, and what each person is willing to trade.

But the framing of “personal preference” only works if all the options lead to roughly equivalent experiences. They do not. A student walking ten minutes from Daehak-dong to the business school building and a student spending hours on the subway each day are not simply making different lifestyle choices. They are operating under different constraints, with different access to the small, unscheduled, often unplanned moments that end up mattering.

I can write this article partly because I live in Nokdu-geori. That Wednesday night, I went to the after-party because I could. The walk home after midnight was easy. Proximity is not free; my monthly rent makes that clear enough. But the cost I pay is mostly financial, while others are paying with a currency that cannot be earned back. That is a different kind of burden from spending hours a day on the subway, or sleeping on a department lounge couch so you can make 6 a.m. practice.

At a university where so much happens outside scheduled hours, where you live does more than determine your address. It quietly determines how much of the place you actually get to keep.
