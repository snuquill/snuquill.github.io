During lunch hours at Seoul National University, students assemble at cafeterias and restaurants. The most extensive lines form for the 1,000-won meal (천식), a longstanding program valued for its accessibility. Outside, more groups gather to chat over coffee. This vibrant social scene, however, masks a persistent issue: students encounter the same, repetitive meal options every day, leading many to avoid campus dining altogether. While SNU prides itself on being one of South Korea’s premier educational institutions, it must reassess whether its dining system—often projected as nutritious, affordable, and diverse—truly supports student well-being.

**What Are Students Really Paying For?**

Scrutiny towards the quality of cafeteria food has been documented as far back as the 1990s. In 1997, emeritus professor Lee Chang Woo wrote an article advocating for higher standards in campus meals while maintaining affordability.

Those same concerns persist today, even after the introduction of the 1000-won meal program in 2015. This plan follows a predictable rotation of stir-fried vegetables or stews. Protein portions remain scarce, appearing occasionally as fish cake balls, small rib patties, or fried chicken bites. One Chemistry student (class of 2027) admitted he only eats the 1,000-won meal because it is cheap. “I usually get hungry again after three hours,” he said. When asked about the menu, he added, “I think it's mostly the rice that fills me up. The main dishes usually come with only four or five pieces on the side.”

The World Health Organization recommends a diet that includes a variety of vegetables, grains, and proteins while limiting foods high in salt, sugar, and saturated fat. Yet many 1,000-won dishes rely on processed foods and sodium-heavy stews with little to no vegetable content. While SNU maintains that the program provides nutritious meals at a low cost, it seems that dietary variety is often more of a promotional projection than a daily reality.

Beyond the 1,000-won option, standard menu prices have risen without corresponding improvements in quality. In April 2022, SNUCO increased meal prices by 25%, moving from the 3,000 to 6,000 won range to the 4,000 to 7,000 won range. Despite promises to use the increase to enhance meal quality, many students have complained that standards remain unchanged.

Some may recall the viral post about the *bossam* meal from a few years ago. Even with the student discount, the dish was 7,000-won and consisted of only a few slices of boiled pork and a bowl of soybean stew. As portions remain small and dissatisfaction grows, the price hikes become increasingly difficult to justify.

These cafeteria shortcomings fit into a broader pattern across campus dining. From the main entrance to the campus heights, the alternatives are just as limited: convenience stores or fast-food franchises. Because only large businesses can afford the rent and overhead required to operate on campus, the resulting lack of diversity remains a serious issue.

**No Place at the Table: The Case for Vegetarians**

The problems with campus dining extend beyond price and quality. While SNU attempts to incorporate variety based on student needs, specific dietary requirements remain underrepresented. Though the university began offering vegetarian meals in 2010 as part of its globalization efforts, vegetarian students today are met with minimal options.

One vegetarian exchange student shared that she could not find proper vegetarian food at all on campus. She either eats off-campus or substitutes lunch with tea or coffee until dinner. “There’s really nothing I can eat,” she explained. “I usually don’t eat eggs either, but since the salad restaurant is the only place that offers egg salad and some other non-meat options, that’s the best I can do.”

While cafeterias do include vegetable-based dishes, these dishes are inconsistently offered and often limited to the 1,000-won option. They are typically made up of stir-fried mushrooms and steamed rice. Even specialty locations like Preppers Diet Food do not offer fully vegan options, as meals usually include meat-based proteins. To this day, the struggle of vegetarian students to navigate campus food shows that SNU’s projection of inclusivity functions more as a symbolic gesture than a genuine commitment.

**The Triumph of Profit-making**

One must then ask why a university aiming at diversity fails to accommodate its students. The issue lies not only in institutional negligence but in practical constraints. Campus dining decisions are heavily influenced by demand, and when faced with financial limitations, the university and its contractors must choose what can be served sustainably. The vegetarian restaurant was closed because the number of diners was not enough for it to remain profitable. This suggests a deeper issue: a system where profit-making is the priority. The university thus designs dining plans “for students” without taking their actual needs properly into consideration.

Mass-produced meals prioritize maximizing profit through large-scale sales, leaving little room for nutritious or diverse options. The 1,000-won program, constrained by the need for affordability and the unpredictable nature of donations, struggles with financial planning. Consequently, meals are designed to serve the largest number of people at the lowest possible cost, often resulting in students receiving what is available in bulk rather than what is balanced or satisfying.

When campus dining becomes something to endure rather than enjoy, it reflects how the university views its community. If the institution prioritizes efficiency over experience, it understands the student body as mere numbers to be managed rather than people deserving of care. Encountering limited options and low-quality daily, students learn that their well-being is often secondary to financial convenience.

**Limited Choice, Limited Voice**

The role of the students in this system is that of “constrained actors.” Although meals are often repetitive and of low quality, many still accept them because they must. Students on tight budgets choose campus dining as it is the most affordable and convenient option. They deprioritize nutrition in favor of saving time and money. These choices are driven by economic necessity, not preference—a rational trade-off, not an endorsement of the system.

Evidence of dissatisfaction exists on SNUCO’s suggestion board (한마디 제안), where students express discontent regarding meal quality and price increases. Yet these scattered, individual complaints rarely translate into substantial pressure for change. The cycle of students depending on a meal system that prioritizes cost continues as they lack the collective power to demand better.

**A Way Forward**

Improvement, however, is not entirely out of reach. SNU can still live up to its promises of a supportive meal system by genuinely considering students’ dietary needs. The university must step back and adjust the structure currently built on assumed preferences and cost-effectiveness.

On top of certain consistent rotations, SNU could introduce seasonal options—such as autumn roasted vegetables or winter hot pots—and international cuisines like Thai curry or Mexican rice bowls. Cafeterias should guarantee at least one fully vegetarian meal daily at every site. Transparency is also key; the university could provide monthly disclosures breaking down ingredient costs, labor, and operational expenses. This would involve regular feedback sessions where SNUCO responds to student suggestions with specific implementations.

Students, too, must move beyond individual complaints to demand collective change. Surveys documenting dissatisfaction, organized petitions, and advocacy from the student council are necessary to drive dining reforms.

These solutions require institutional investment, but they are essential to transforming the campus eating experience. Dining is not just about filling stomachs. When students cannot eat well on campus, the university is failing its basic responsibility to care for them. Only by listening to student voices can SNU move beyond a mere projection of care and create an environment that students want to remain in.
