Twice a year, the Grass Square at Seoul National University transforms into festival grounds. Rows of tents rise, banners flutter in the breeze, and music fills the air as students call out to those passing by. This is *Dongari Sogaeje* (동아리 소개제), SNU’s club recruitment fair, marking the start of another busy semester. Students wander between booths, collecting flyers and watching performances, inquiring into the many communities that thrive beyond lecture halls.

This enthusiasm doesn’t fade when the tents come down. From performances to publications, the campus continues to buzz as intangible ambition is transformed into visible proof. On fields and courts, department teams train for weeks ahead of intramural tournaments—practicing at dawn, determined to win.

These scenes thus reveal something distinctive about life at SNU: extracurriculars are not just hobbies, but extensions of a culture where excellence is the standard. But beneath the pride and productivity lies a question: why does everything—even recreation—feel like a test? Perhaps the answer can be found through a deep dive into SNU’s club culture, where ambition and belonging pull against each other, yet remain inseparable. 

**Commitment Beyond Lecture Halls**

At SNU, joining a club often means more than just filling free time. For many students, club membership shapes their university life and sometimes even their careers. Recruitment feels more like job placement than choosing a hobby, complete with applications, interviews, and multiple rounds of evaluation. Once accepted, the rhythm is relentless: weekly meetings, deadlines, and a quiet pressure to prove that you belong. Club participation feels like unpaid training, another arena where diligence becomes identity and identity becomes a projection of competence.

This phenomenon is clearest in academic societies, or *hakhoe* (학회). Originally meant to help students explore interests beyond class, many *hakhoe* now operate like professional organizations. Whether in business, engineering, or the sciences, members prepare for competitions, run consulting projects, or lead seminars that resemble graduate-level work. Late-night rehearsals and back-to-back presentations blur the line between extracurriculars and actual “job prep.” The pride in one’s belonging comes not only from the work itself, but from being seen as the kind of student who can handle it. I used to admire that level of commitment; now I wonder if we sometimes mistake exhaustion for purpose.

Creative and performance-based clubs follow a similar logic. Dance and theater teams rehearse for weeks, and bands practice relentlessly before performing in major concert halls. Spaces meant for exploration often feel like auditions for a professional stage that students haven’t yet reached. That pressure is clearest in clubs that actually require physical auditions, like hip-hop crews or dance collectives. These clubs are supposed to be homes for passion, yet to join, students must already seem like they have been honing their craft for countless years. This creates a hierarchy where the more rigorous the audition process, the “better” the club seems. As Choi, a junior and devoted hip-hop fan, told me, “I got rejected by every club I applied to for three straight semesters. I know I have the passion, but they don’t think I’m a good fit—not even with a small crew.”

That being said, not everyone joins clubs out of love for the activities they promote. Many chase the “right” names. As a result, smaller, niche clubs often fade away quietly, while “prestigious” ones overflow with applicants. When everything feels like a line in the resume, sincerity becomes a luxury. What matters is less the experience itself than what it signals: capability, connection, and constant upward movement.

Within South Korea’s hierarchy of higher education, SNU sits at the top, a place where students must continuously prove their potential. Clubs mirror that mindset. They become extensions of the university’s culture of excellence, where the status of belonging often counts for more than the purpose of participation. What unsettles me most is how easily we measure our own worth through association.

Still, there are students who join smaller, less-exclusive circles out of a genuine love for the game and the culture of the club. Yet even in those spaces, rigorous discipline seeps in. At SNU, play itself needs a schedule.

This mindset is especially visible on the fields and courts. Every semester, departmental teams compete fiercely in intramural tournaments, including football, basketball, baseball, and tennis, cheered on by classmates and alumni. The training may be informal, but the will to improve—to win—feels entirely characteristic of SNU.

I've seen it for myself with ARMADA, the Business Administration football club. No one gets paid, and there’s nothing material to gain, but we train hard anyway. Practices begin when the field is still slick with dew, and every match feels like the last. Playing as a center-back for ARMADA, I also train daily in the gym or on the field just to keep up. There’s a constant sense of not wanting to fall behind, to guard your position on the starting eleven.

What struck me the most about ARMADA wasn’t just the level of commitment to football, but the depth of investment in the club itself. We train together, study together, and spend long hours together off the field. Over time, I’ve caught myself becoming an “ARMADA-type,” shaped not only by what I do but by the people I do it with. Evidently, at SNU, identity often takes form in close proximity to the clubs students belong to.

That collective effort paid off this semester when ARMADA won the SNU Football Cup. The victory felt like the earned result of months of bruises, early mornings, and late-night drills. When the final whistle blew, some players collapsed in relief while others burst into tears. Even those who hadn’t played a single minute were crying on the sidelines. The celebration was raw and unfiltered, a reflection of how much this club means to its members.
Whether students chase prestige, passion, or something in between, their devotion to club life points to a shared instinct: to treat every commitment like a competition, and every competition like a statement. At SNU, even leisure seems to carry purpose. It’s not just pressure, but habit—a belief that effort, or at least the appearance of it, is what gives things their value.

**Living Up to the Name**

The instinct to push harder and do more—even outside academics—is rarely questioned at SNU. Years in a hierarchical school system have made comparison second nature. That inclination doesn’t vanish once students enter university. It simply changes form.

This expectation extends beyond *hakhoe* to all types of extracurriculars. Performance-based clubs work to uphold the standards set by previous generations. Sports teams feel pressure to maintain winning records. Central clubs strive to outdo the impact of earlier cohorts. Every club carries a legacy, and students feel compelled to at least match—or surpass—the projection of success that came before.

Still, *hakhoe* culture makes this logic especially visible. Late-night rehearsals, blunt feedback, and unspoken hierarchies resemble the corporate world students are preparing to enter. In fields like business and economics, *hakhoe* names appear on resumes like unofficial certifications. Alumni networks form through them, recruiters recognize their prestige, and those outside these circles feel small before they even apply. 

That sense of constant measurement is present beyond the clubs themselves. Students often admit feeling uneasy when they sense that they’re falling behind, not necessarily in grades, but in experiences, awards, or recognition. “When someone posts about winning a competition or hosting an event, you can’t help but think, ‘What am I doing with my life?’” one student said half-jokingly. I’ve caught myself doing the same, comparing my calendar to someone else’s as if having free time were a flaw that needed fixing.

The competitiveness even bleeds into anonymous spaces. On *SNU Everytime*, the university’s online forum, comments about clubs and achievements are often biting: “That *hakhoe* isn’t even that good,” or “People who didn’t get in probably just didn’t try hard enough.” The posts read like jokes, but they carry pride mixed with insecurity. We laugh, yet the words sting because there’s a consistent grain of truth—no one wants to feel left behind. Detachment becomes a mask; irony becomes self-defense.

Maybe that’s why no one questions the pace anymore. Working hard is the only way we know how to belong, or at least appear as though we do. Even in practice fields and dance studios, the same energy prevails—training before dawn, rehearsing until midnight. From the outside, it might look excessive, but from within, it feels like the norm. Competition at SNU isn't only about outperforming others; it's about living up to an invisible standard of being overqualified, disciplined, and endlessly striving for more. 

**Between Passion and Performance**

When the semester ends, only traces of club life remain across campus—torn posters, messy rehearsal rooms, and group chats that suddenly go silent. For many, these clubs were stages where effort doubled as identity. Relief often mixes in with the pride of accomplishment, as the academic calendar forces the busy rhythm to pause for the time being. 

That double edge defines SNU: the same system that leads to burnout also forges a sense of belonging. What stands out is not just how hard students work, but how much of that effort is tied to recognition. People stay late to help teammates, cheer on friends, or pour themselves into projects that no one outside their club will ever notice. The work is genuine, even if the desire to be seen exists beneath it. 

In the end, SNU’s clubs mirror the university itself: a place where excellence is both a pursuit and a performance. Every practice and presentation carries the instinct to affirm that such intensive investment means something. We call it passion, but maybe it’s also projection—a compulsion to appear tireless, capable, and certain, even when we’re not. As I walk past the empty field where the recruitment tents once stood, I'm reminded of how quickly participation becomes expectation. At SNU, effort isn’t just what we do. It’s who we learn to look like.
