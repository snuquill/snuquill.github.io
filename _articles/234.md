Picture a wintry January morning in Seoul, 2026\. Before it’s even open, a long queue has already formed outside a small dessert shop tucked inside a Seongsu-dong alley between a coffee roaster and a vintage clothing store. Near the front, a university student in a padded coat scrolls through Instagram on her phone as she stamps her feet against the cold. When the door finally opens, the entire day’s batch of *dujjonku* vanishes within minutes. Those who are too late quickly open specialized maps that track which shops still have stock of the delicacy.

What is *dujjonku*? Combining pistachio cream with kadaif, a Middle Eastern pastry, results in a sweet, crunchy filling, which is then wrapped inside a melted marshmallow skin dusted with cocoa powder. This produces the Dubai chewy cookie (두바이 쫀득 쿠키), or *dujjonku* for short. Its elasticity resembles Korean *tteok*, or ricecake, blending foreign flavors with a familiar texture.

Originally invented in April 2025 as a reinterpretation of the Dubai chocolate fad of 2024, *dujjonku* gained immense popularity when famous K-pop idol Jang Wonyoung posted herself tasting the dessert in September 2025\. This sparked a *dujjonku* frenzy, resulting in nearly every bakery beginning to sell the item. Somehow, the supply still failed to meet the spike in demand, causing *dujjonku* prices to skyrocket to more than 8,000 won per unit at one point. Furthermore, pistachio prices surged 400%, and marshmallow supplies dried up from not only commercial sellers but consumers trying to replicate the recipe at home. According to CU, their convenience stores sold 1.8 million units in three months.

### **The Same Story, New Wrapper**

The *dujjonku* craze is eerily familiar. It was honey-butter chips in 2014, *fatcarons* in 2018, and tanghulu in 2023\. *Dujjonku* is only the latest manifestation of Korea’s “food fads”—a cultural phenomenon almost ferocious in velocity and volume. Each of these fads share a recognizable lifecycle. An exotic origin outside the mainstream (for example, Chinese street food, a European delicacy, or in the case of *dujjonku*, a Middle Eastern snack) piques Korean consumers’ curiosity. Then a K-pop idol or influencer ignites the boom. People flood the shops, and the following scarcity of the product itself drives the craze. Finally, as the market catches up and supply becomes sufficient to satisfy demand, the bubble pops.

Lining past fads up, one can find an interesting trend: acceleration. *Fatcarons* held their throne for about two years. Tanghulu skewers lined streets for about 12 months. The *dujjonku* craze burned bright for only six. The intervals between fads are shrinking, and now trends disappear right after everybody has caught up; rather than the product, the catching-on itself is the content. The platforms that amplify food fads—for example, social media such as Instagram—now move so fast that what once sustained a trend has been replaced by near simultaneous national saturation.

Through this frame of dessert crazes, we peer into Korea’s society: how Instagram and FOMO inflames trends into virality; how Gen-Z mainly consumes the experience and shareability, not the product itself; and how, rather than being a coincidence, these food fads might be a product of an economic recession bearing down on the current generation.

### **Instagrammability & FOMO**

*Dujjonku* and other desserts that have historically gained popularity in Korea share a key trait: striking visuals. Bursting *fatcarons* stuffed to the brim with filling; shiny, rainbow-colored tanghulu; and now *dujjonku*, with the eye-catching contrast of the chewy chocolate marshmallow shell and bright green pistachio-kadaif mixture oozing out—all appeal visually before they appeal gustatorily, making them perfect content for flashy Instagram stories. The “stretch shot” with two hands pulling the cookie apart until the marshmallow stretches into mere threads became its own genre on Korean Instagram, generating millions of views—essentially being the home-grown Korean counterpart of the viral “cheese pull” trend.

Social media and bandwagon consumption are not unique to Korea. However, having one of the world's most densely populated urban spheres and being one of the most digitally connected societies, Korea is a space where social media trends can spread at breakneck speed. The unique culture of Korea also plays a role. As Professor Han Jisang of Sunkyunkwan University put it, “Korea’s collectivist culture is a factor that spreads trends quickly. As one product gains popularity, you get the pressure to ‘catch on,’ leading to explosive growth.” The pressure to keep up with trends is less about individual taste than it is about social belonging—the sense of participating in the same cultural moment as everybody else in my Instagram feed. The fear of missing out is real. The queues outside bakeries are not merely a quest for a sweet treat, but for a sense of membership.

### **Consuming the Experience, not the Food**

While standing in the cold for hours for a single snack is somewhat counterintuitive, the discomfort itself is constitutive of the *dujjonku* experience: it is not a bug, but a feature. The waiting, scarcity, and the highly anticipated bite all combine to form a narrative that can be shared, a story in which the buyer is not only a consumer but also a content creator. 

As food critic Lee Yongjae put it: “We don’t care how it tastes; the queueing itself is the product.”

Experience economy has become a concept central to understanding contemporary customer behavior. As goods and services become abundant, the product itself ceases to become a differentiator. Instead, what becomes scarce, unique, and valuable is the experience attached to it. Gen Z, who have the highest social media usage in the country, drive this trend of spending for content and shareability. The experience is unique, yet shared by millions; the value comes in you doing *what* everyone else is doing *when* everybody is doing it. Therefore, the fad must be brief. The memory is valuable not because of the snack’s long-lasting flavor, but because it is a shared cultural moment unique to that specific winter.

An interesting comparison can be drawn with another craze that shook the world, but somehow never reached *dujjonku*\-level intensity in Korea. The similarities between Labubus—a Pop-Mart toy that exploded globally in 2024—and *dujjonku* are striking: both ignited by a K-pop singer’s Instagram, priced at a comparable range, also built on scarcity and FOMO. However, compared with Labubus, which is an object, storable and static, the experience one gets from consuming *dujjonku* is more immersive, multisensory, and instantaneous. The experience is more valuable precisely because it is a fleeting moment  and the product disappears once consumed.

**The Lipstick Effect**

At first glance, it might be strange that cookies priced at a whopping 8,000 won apiece sold like hot cakes—“in this economy?” South Korea’s consumer economy from 2024 to 2025 was sluggish. The composite customer sentiment index dropped 2.5 points in January, and 53% of respondents said they planned to reduce spending in a National Consumption Expenditure Survey. The employment rate of young adults was at an all time low, dropping for 17 consecutive months. It was against this backdrop that the *dujjonku* craze kicked in.

The lipstick effect, coined by Leonard Lauder when his high-end lipstick brand sales rose as the economy fell after the 9/11 terrorist attacks, proposes that consumers in economic stress substitute large luxuries for relatively affordable ones, instead of cutting spending entirely. In contemporary Korea, it seems that desserts priced at 6,000 to 10,000 won are the equivalent of Lauder’s lipstick. This aligns neatly with the Korean concept of *sohwakhaeng*—small but certain happiness. It feels impossible for the younger generation to climb up the social ladder as previous generations did: wealth is concentrated amongst a select few, the future is unstable, and there is little hope of making enough money even in a lifetime to buy a house in Seoul. So, instead, one turns toward the bakery where for 8,000 won, you can get a small but sure delight that still makes you feel elite.

### **Post-Dujjonku**

By February 2026, the craze had all but passed. *Dujjonku* bakery owner Elly Moon said her daily sales were about 10 to 15, half of what it used to be in January. “We need to keep developing other desserts or inventing new things,” she said. Next is *bomdong-bibimbap*, then *butter-tteok*, new items replacing the same slot in the same frame.

More than just a social phenomenon, the *dujjonku* fad has had real effects on small businesses. Shops that scaled up in size and production during the peak are left vulnerable when demand collapses just as rapidly as it arrived. The craze benefits early adopters, while punishing those who join late. An accelerating cycle time means less time for adjustment, less time to brace for the shock.

Korea’s food fads are not merely a consumer caprice; they reveal deeper undercurrents in Korea’s society. Amidst a hyper-connected digital culture, collectivist social pressures, and an economy where large aspirations feel out of reach, these food fads are an expression of this generation’s needs. For 8,000 won and an hour of waiting, it delivers the feeling of being at the center of things, of being connected with everybody else.

The cycle will continue because the conditions producing it have not changed. The apartments in Seoul will not suddenly become affordable next year. The job market will not magically absorb the next graduating class. Instagram will continue to be central to how an entire generation forms their identity. And so the frame persists, waiting to be filled—by *bomdong-bimbimbap*, by *butter-tteok*, by whatever arrives next from somewhere exotic, blessed by somebody famous, photographed from just the right angle. The queue will re-form outside some other shop in some other alley. The question worth asking is not what the next *dujjonku* will be, but what kind of society keeps needing one.
