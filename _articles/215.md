It’s common knowledge that gambling is illegal in South Korea. Moreover, Korean nationals are prohibited from engaging in gambling anywhere in the world, regardless of local legality. But what if, despite strict regulations, gambling…*wasn’t* illegal?
 
This isn’t about *Kangwon Land*, the sole casino open to Koreans. This also isn’t about the foreigner-only casinos, or even the horse racing and lottery. No, this is about the thousands of crane game arcades filling the streets of Hongdae, the hordes of people lining up outside Popmart for a chance at buying a Labubu, and the billions spent on in-game microtransactions.
 
South Korean law defines the gambling industry as including casinos, horse racing, bicycle racing, motorboat racing, the lottery, sports betting, and bullfighting. Under this definition, forms of pseudo-gambling such as crane games, in-game microtransactions, and blind boxes are allowed to slip through. While these modest acts of spending seem innocent at first glance, they have a tendency to snowball into significant sums of money before you even realize it. The accessibility of pseudo-gambling is particularly an issue for vulnerable members of society, such as adolescents without a strong grasp of rational spending. It creates a potential gateway to a full-blown gambling addiction in the future. A societal shift in perspective is clearly necessary—pseudo-gambling should be treated as an equivalent to “real gambling.”
 
**Gambling: You’re Already Part of the Problem**
 
The popularization of social media might not be the source of every problem encountered by the youth, but it plays a big part in the case of pseudo-gambling. Every couple of weeks, a new microtrend surfaces online; influencers, in their desperation to remain relevant, are inclined to address each and every one of them. Whether they choose to endorse or criticize, they bring attention to these trends. Ultimately, that attention boosts circulation through the algorithm and spreads awareness of microtrends to a greater audience. All press is good press after all.
 
Take Labubus as an example—the highly coveted doll keychain series that has generated over 670 million USD in 2025 alone. The dolls are constantly shrouded in controversy. Many rightfully point out the overconsumption it promotes. Meanwhile, those same people scroll through blind box opening hauls on TikTok, participating vicariously in the same cycle. Labubus continue to fly off the shelves as the randomized nature of the dolls encourages repeat sales. The people invested in buying these dolls aim to complete a collection or obtain the design they want with the same obsessive nature as gamblers at a slot machine—repeatedly trying their luck in pursuit of a desired outcome, only to end up with unwanted duplicates along the way. What’s worse is that companies such as Popmart have been creating artificial scarcities. Using buzzwords like “limited edition,” they trick people into compulsively buying blind boxes on sight. The commercial success of Labubus then naturally brings attention to other forms of blind boxes, giving more life to the pseudo-gambling industry.
 
This newfound gambling epidemic has also already infected the streets. Simply taking a stroll around any youth-saturated area in Seoul shows how omnipresent gambling is in our lives. According to *The Korea Times* in September 2025, the Game Rating and Administration Committee reported a 20% increase in crane game arcades, which brought the total up to over 5,900. Crane game arcades are escaping their parent establishments of traditional arcades and are being set up as standalones. In the remaining traditional arcades, crane game machines tend to take up the majority of space because they bring in the most money. After all, it’s so much easier to make a quick detour to grab a cute trinket than to sit down and play an actual game.
 
It’s not just crane games. *Gashapon*, or capsule toys, are also at the center of this gambling epidemic. What’s even worse with capsule toys is that they promise that you’ll never leave empty-handed. This traps consumers into a false sense of security that they are not wasting their money. These machines are far too approachable with their simple mechanics and low cost of use, and the stock of trendy, cutesy items clearly indicates that its audience is young and impressionable.
 
**The Chips Are Down for the Youth**
 
That’s not to say that indulging yourself with a cute new plushie or keychain is inherently bad. The underlying issue is the way new, flashy collectibles are constantly being promoted to vulnerable members of society. These forms of pseudo-gambling are mostly geared toward a younger audience who may not be the most financially literate. Since the prefrontal cortex, the center for impulse control and rational thinking, is still underdeveloped in adolescents, this demographic is more prone to addiction as a whole. In the case of pseudo-gambling, the smaller sums of money involved in a single transaction make it an approachable and appealing pastime.
 
Arguably, the perceived severity of pseudo-gambling in South Korea is further diminished by the colloquialization of the term “dopamine.” It has quickly entered the lexicon of many young people as shorthand for any quick thrill or moment of excitement, easily justifying pseudo-gambling highs. “Chasing dopamine” is encouraged, and one of the easiest ways to do so is through these addictive mechanics.
 
This brings us to the biggest problem with pseudo-gambling. It’s in the name itself. Pseudo-gambling isn’t treated as actual gambling. Gambling has been successfully villainized through horror stories of individuals losing their possessions, damaging their relationships beyond repair, and effectively ruining their entire lives. Publicized studies further highlight its fatally addictive nature. While traditional casino gambling is surrounded by stigma that successfully deters many people from engaging, pseudo-gambling lacks such a mark. It is seen as harmless, whether due to the appearance of low risk and low cost involved, the younger audience it caters towards, or the concept of indirectly gambling for a “prize” rather than money. As such, people tend to dismiss the potential dangers; there are no official efforts to expose its dark side.
 
However, the American Psychological Association (APA) reports that the core mechanics behind pseudo-gambling and traditional gambling are the same: “an exchange of real money for valuable goods with an unknown outcome determined at least partly by chance.” Increasingly, this is becoming a worldwide phenomenon that poses a clear risk to our youth. A startling 20% of gamblers in the UK have admitted that their introduction to gambling was through pseudo-gambling in video games, with more than 80% having started as minors. Canadian research on young adult video gamers also found that many transitioned to actual gambling after only six months.
 
A 2019 survey conducted on behalf of Popmart, the company behind Labubus, found that “around 70% of pop toy consumers purchase the same blind box toy three or more times for a specific toy design they want.” Regardless of the outcome, people find themselves craving the instant gratification of spending and opening packages. This proves to be especially dangerous for individuals with mental health issues. In a *Guardian* article from August 2025, one interviewee reported that blind boxes were a perfect quick fix “to have that little bit of serotonin or dopamine” following a manic episode; she reflected that her mental health was the hardest hit during the peak of this addiction. Furthermore, a 2023 study found a positive correlation between blind box consumption and a tendency to engage in actual gambling. Evidently, the seemingly harmless blind boxes can lead to detrimental long-term effects, especially when children are exposed to them from a young age.
 
**A Gamble for Change**
 
This isn’t to say that attempts to regulate the pseudo-gambling industry are non-existent. For example, a 2023 amendment to the Game Industry Promotion Act requiring all games to disclose the probabilities behind *gacha* items prompted industry giant EA to block microtransactions in several of its titles in South Korea. Additionally, claw machine operators must submit manuals detailing machine specifications and gameplay methods to the Game Rating and Administration Committee, which ensures the mechanics are not rigged. However, these laws are poorly enforced, as evidenced by the high volume of consumer complaints each year. It is an open secret that the claws in these machines are designed to release prizes deliberately, only tightening after a programmed number of attempts. Clearly, this isn’t *enough*.
 
The first and most crucial step is awareness, specifically recognizing pseudo-gambling not as a separate category, but a variant of gambling itself. Despite its softer label, pseudo-gambling utilizes the same risk-reward mechanics, dragging individuals deeper into an inescapable rabbit hole while remaining largely unchecked. This is what makes pseudo-gambling an especially perfect gateway to more severe forms of gambling for the youth. The trivialization of dopamine overexposure inherent to gambling and its associations with gaming and adolescence shields the practice from parental and regulatory scrutiny. Only by shifting the way society perceives this phenomenon can we expose its true dangers.
 
This is not to suggest that participants are unaware of the nature of these pseudo-gambling activities;  many already joke about the fact that they are, in effect, gambling. It’s also common knowledge that despite the government’s efforts—or perhaps their turning of a blind eye—crane games are likely rigged against the player. And yet, people keep coming back for more. How else can this persistence be explained, if not as a plain, simple *gambling addiction*? It’s time to stop tiptoeing around the issue and call it what it is.
