**A War Against Cats**  
Sometime during middle school, I suggested to my friend that the government should kill all stray cats and was promptly labelled a psychopath. At the time, I believed that I was making a rational and salient point on the preservation of urban nature. Feral cats have been named among the 100 worst invasive species by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) and are responsible for the complete extinction of at least 63 distinct animal species across the world. Among those 63 species, a whopping 40 of them are birds. Middle school me, who held a particular fascination towards birds, thought this was the greatest tragedy on earth. She believed she bore the unenviable responsibility of “enlightening” her cat-loving friend about the necessity of burying the things you love, whether that be feral cats or a 10-year-long friendship. In simple terms, I was an insufferable nerd.  
It turns out I was ahead of my time, because there has been an uptick in online conversations around so-called “cat moms”—people who take care of the neighborhood cats by feeding them and funding their veterinarian care. Cat moms are widely bashed across the Korean internet landscape for the inconvenience they cause within residential areas: more cats in the streets leads to more noise, roadkill, and poor hygiene. According to a 2025 investigation in Seoul, 94.8% of cat food was found in densely populated areas such as low-rise apartments, leading to more civil complaints from residents.  
While this conflict between cat moms and frustrated residents has always been prevalent, a new faction has joined the war in recent years: birdwatching enthusiasts. Birders point out that cats are the leading cause behind the endangerment of birds, so the government must control their population through whatever means necessary. And the government is indeed responding to their increasing demands; in March this year, the Ministry of Rural Affairs introduced the “stray cat care guidelines” stating that cat food cannot be placed on private property without the landowner’s permission. Cities are also introducing TNR (Trap, Neuter, and Return) programs to gradually phase out the cat population. Recently, however, wildlife conservationists are arguing that TNR cannot meaningfully reduce the harm. In order to eradicate a feral cat colony through TNR alone, at least 70% of the population should be neutered—an unmanageable scale even in countries with harsher protection standards, such as Australia. Consequently, birders are increasingly pushing for a stricter focus on euthanizing the cats rather than releasing them back into the wild.  
I am unfortunately a nerdy psychopath still. I like birdwatching (lame), and I agree that our native birds must be protected through every available means; if TNR-ing cats demonstrably fail, we must begin exploring other methods. At the same time, I find myself hesitating to side with my natural allies in this war against cat moms. Because before entering political discourse on the internet, there is one question you must ask yourself: why are these valiant basement dwellers fighting alongside you? Why are they attacking cat people on social media instead of joining more worthwhile pursuits? And once you begin, it’s difficult to pull that train of questions off track: Why are these people insistent on calling their enemies by a pointlessly gendered moniker? Why are they a bit *too* happy about these cats getting killed? Are these people…chuds?

**Two Divergent Battles for Citizenship**  
	Doubtless it is grossly reductive to claim that every single person who engages with this topic is a bad faith actor who doesn’t actually care about protecting birds. In fact I would say the opposite: wildlife conservation is deeply rooted in caring about animals as sentient beings who should be afforded the same dignity and respect as ourselves. American Ornithologist Florence Merriam Bailey, who helped popularize birdwatching as a hobby in 19th century America, addresses the birds in her field guide *Birds Through an Opera Glass* with a distinctly courteous voice. She writes, for example, that even a bird as unassuming as the robin “bespeaks the self-respecting American citizen.” This equivalency she draws between the robin and the “American citizen” marks a clear transition from past attitudes in the 18th and early 19th century, where protection of animals was seen as a moral duty to care for less sentient beings (as per Jean-Jacques Rousseau), or as a simple economic duty to guard one’s property. These earlier approaches towards conservation worked under the assumption that animals are far less rational than humans, and thus fit to be unilaterally advocated *for,* not *with.* Merriam, however, refers to the robin as a “citizen” to not only compare it to a fellow human being, but also to indicate that nature deserves a more equal standing in America’s civic and political sphere. “Robin” implies that the robin is a silly little guy. “Citizen” implies that the robin should get to line up at the voting booth or run for the mayor of a small city.  
	Perhaps I am vastly overrepresenting the extent to which Merriam wanted to politicize the robin. It may very well be that she simply wanted to emphasize the importance of wildlife to America’s national identity. Nevertheless, Merriam’s compassionate diction was reflective of the emergence of wildlife conservation as a broader egalitarian movement. In the late 19th century, the Western fashion industry was profiting massively from the sale of feathered hats for middle and upper-class women. Hunters culled millions of birds each year to feed the public’s appetite for poofy hats—at one point, the industry threatened at least 61 species of hummingbirds to extinction. Seeing this, a small group of British women founded the Society for the Protection of Birds (SPB) and called for the illegalization of violent hunting practices in the plumage trade. At first the SPB was met with skepticism and ridicule. The British Parliament dismissed their demands, stating that “the lives of human beings(…)are more important than the lives of birds.” Soon, however, they were soon joined by similar activist groups across the world such as the Audubon Society in America (which was in fact founded by Florence Merriam). Together, these conservationists pioneered the “Murderous Millinery” movement, fighting to dethrone man from his self-appointed position as master of nature. The conservationists’ concerns extended beyond a mere sentimental stint: at the movement’s core lay the political understanding that humans were no longer the center of the universe.  
	As popular support grew, however, the Murderous Millinery movement began to gain some questionable proponents. These supporters mobilized the public against women who wore feathered hats, labelling them “bird-enemies” and “fashion-mad harpies.” Political cartoons satirized these women as heartless, bestial creatures who killed innocent wildlife for their personal pleasure. But the most fascinating allies of them all were the anti-suffragettes. The burgeoning revolutionary notion that the old dominant class must be decentered didn’t just spur on the conservationists; it motivated the women’s suffrage movement as well, which coincided with the Murderous Millinery movement. Although both were created in response to the dominance of Man (in both senses of the word), their alignments couldn’t be more different in practice: the suffragettes preferred to rally in huge feathered hats to signify their right to occupy physical and sociopolitical “space”—a deliberate strategy that the conservationists vehemently opposed. What resulted was a complex interaction between two women-led movements that was mostly defined by hostility. Hostility which was, rather unsurprisingly, taken advantage of by organizations such as the Men’s League for Opposing Women’s Suffrage. While it is hard to determine how much meaningful cooperation existed between anti-suffragettes and conservationists, it’s difficult to deny that their arguments, either intentionally or inadvertently, echoed each other by appealing to traditional sentiments of motherhood. The Men’s League disparaged the decadently dressed suffragettes for neglecting their womanly duties at home, while the Audubon Society criticized that fashion enthusiasts were going against their natural motherly roles by condoning the orphaning of egret chicks.

**The Infiltrators, One Century Later**  
	Both sides of the war got what they wanted eventually, roughly around the same time. While the Murderous Millinery movement lost steam for a while as World War I came and went, it did reach its hard-won culmination in 1921 when the Importation of Plumage Prohibition Bill was passed in British Parliament. The Women’s Suffrage Movement’s success was more gradual, with two successive bills in 1918 and 1928 granting voting rights to all women over 21\. Moreover, the two sides managed to reconcile and even merge together in the form of Ecofeminism—an ecocentrist branch of the women’s rights movement that prioritizes equality for all citizens of the natural world, not just men and women. The prominence of Ecofeminism today makes it difficult to imagine that wildlife conservationists and feminists once stood against each other on the metaphorical battlefield. It also makes it easy to erase the meddlers who sowed division for their own political ends.  
	These same meddlers are now rearing their heads in Korea. It should be telling that when you Google “cat mom,” the first autocomplete phrase that pops up is “cat mom getting schooled.” Such is the inevitable cancer that emerges in every political discourse that propagates itself through the internet—people seeking the gratifying thrill that comes with deriding others. What makes them truly harmful is their ability to hijack legitimate and necessary conversations in order to serve their strange, misinformed, and altogether petty causes. Am I expected to believe that these people are truly concerned with the local wildlife? These people who thoughtlessly exploit the long-standing cultural image of the undesirable, unproductive “cat woman,” who can’t come up with an insult without slapping on the affix “-mom?” At what point are they heeding their urge to attack over their obligation to protect and sympathize?  
	Progress in wildlife conservation in 19th century Europe came only with the recognition that all sentient beings deserve the same empathy—meaning that we should, unfortunately, treat these internet trolls as people. Although a simplistic interpretation can be tempting, human beings are rarely demonized caricatures; I would be surprised if these hijackers, including the anti-suffragettes who sided with the conservationists, truly didn’t have an ounce of interest in protecting the natural world. At the same time, human beings are undoubtedly capable of caring about multiple causes at once, with some of these causes being more pressing than others. The historical precedent set by the anti-suffragettes urges conservationists in Korea to reexamine how others are interacting with their movement—whether or not their participation carries the strange brand of chauvinism that permeates Korea’s current sociopolitical landscape. Furthermore, it asks us all to examine whether we are being diverted from what truly matters in favor of more shortsighted pursuits.
