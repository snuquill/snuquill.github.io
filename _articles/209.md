Two exhibitions in Korea this year drew particular attention for foregrounding spirituality and occult imagination. The first was Hilma af Klint: Proper Summons, at the Museum of Contemporary Art Busan, selected by visitors as one of the year’s most noteworthy shows. Though af Klint produced abstract paintings years before Kandinsky, she remained excluded from the modernist canon, largely due to her gender and the spiritualist practices that informed her work. Her recent global reappraisal, reflected in major exhibitions worldwide, signals a reconsideration of what counts within the history of modern art—and the Busan exhibition marks this shift’s arrival in Korea.

The second exhibition was the Seoul Mediacity Biennale, presented under the theme Séance: Technology of the Spirit. The topic may seem unexpected for a biennale long associated with technologically oriented contemporary art, yet curator Anton Vidokle positioned the project as an exploration of “historical and contemporary artistic practices that draw upon occult, mythological, and spiritualist traditions.” Dimly lit galleries created an atmosphere of sanctity, showcasing materials and images long dismissed by canonical art history: drawings influenced by esoteric traditions, works probing mediumship, possession, and spiritual healing, as well as historical spirit photographs and early cinematic experiments with ghostly apparitions. Contemporary pieces addressing collective trauma—such as shamanic rituals related to Jeju’s April 3 massacre—further expanded the exhibition’s scope. Photography and film, presented as media capable of “recalling the dead,” resonated strongly with the biennale’s conceptual frame, as suggested by its title Séance.”

These developments contrast sharply with the modernist tradition, which long marginalized subjects associated with the spiritual or the mystical. Grounded in Enlightenment rationalism, modernist art history privileged a narrative of formal progress and medium-specific autonomy, casting spiritual experience as antithetical to reason and thus unfit for serious artistic discourse. 

The contemporary resurgence of mystical themes is not confined to the art world. Popular culture, too, has witnessed a proliferation of dramas and films featuring shamans, while the recent globally successful animation K-pop Demon Hunters constructs its narrative around Korean shamanism and the exorcism of malevolent spirits.

How might we understand this renewed interest in spirituality, mysticism, and myth in the 21st century? 

German art historian Aby Warburg offers a suggestive framework. Warburg viewed culture as humanity’s ongoing effort to confront and manage primordial fears. He argued that humans interpret the world through two fundamental modes: a mythic-magical worldview and a rational-scientific one. Although modern civilization strengthened the latter, Warburg insisted that history does not unfold along a linear trajectory from myth to logos. Instead, humanity continually oscillates between these poles, negotiating tension and balance in order to sustain a meaningful relationship with nature. He termed this zone of negotiation a “space of thought (Denkraum),” identifying art as a key site where such pendular movement becomes visible. Visual images of any given era reveal how humans relate to the world and concretize the oscillation between myth and logos that shapes human consciousness.

This framework is particularly resonant today. Since the Enlightenment, instrumental reason has shaped the modernization process, promoting the domination of nature and the displacement of mythic thinking. Yet recent ecological crises, the emergence of unpredictable viruses, and global disruptions have exposed the limits of rational control. The resurgence of mythic or spiritual modes of thought may thus reflect not regression but a historical recalibration.

This does not suggest an uncritical return to mysticism. Rather, it signals the need to rethink how humanity navigates the unstable poles of myth and logos. The renewed interest in spiritual imagination—visible in recent exhibitions as well as in broader cultural production—can be understood as part of this negotiation. By revisiting forms of thought once dismissed as irrational, contemporary art opens a space for reevaluating the assumptions of modernity and for reimagining how we might inhabit a world increasingly marked by uncertainty.
