*“Instead of English abbreviations, why not use our language?”* These were the words plastered on the side of train stations across Seoul, encouraging viewers to use the Korean translation of popular English acronyms such as WHO (World Health Organization) and OECD (The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development). They were part of a campaign conducted by the Korean Language Cultural Association in 2022 promoting the use of the Korean language rather than an English-infused *Konglish*, which is often described as taking over the Korean language. Efforts such as these raise a point of contention in Korea: Where does the English language fit in the creation of a Korean identity? Is it inherently part of Korea’s identity negotiation or merely an external contamination—a failure of English?

Efforts to promote the Korean language and suppress English and *Konglish* usage are not a novelty in Korea: the sentiment of English contamination is echoed in both public and government actions. LED subway signs display quizzes prompting commuters to test their Korean, entertainment shows play “no English” games, and bookstores are full of texts on how to use “proper Korean.” In March of this year, the government launched an “Easy Korean Usage” initiative, which reviews the language of published media to ensure that they are using simple and proper language. This is prominently done by eradicating Konglish. Government initiatives starting in 2025 also offered financial compensation (up to ₩2,000,000) for the installation of *Hangul* signs to replace English ones. Furthermore, cases of government-backed English usage are often targets for mass criticism—proposals for Busan to be considered a bilingual city, largely to boost the city’s chances of hosting the 2030 World Expo, drew heavy backlash over concerns of Korean language erasure.

The Korean language has long served as a pillar of national identity, labeled as fundamental to social and economic participation. This poses a pertinent question: is *Konglish* part of Korean identity or, as some describe, a contamination of pure “Koreanness”?

**What is *Konglish* and Does it Belong to Korea?**  
Despite its familiarity and prevalence amongst Koreans, *Konglish* has no formal definition. Some cite it as merely Korean phonetic appropriations of English, others as the product of failed English usage in Korea. This lack of consensus on what *Konglish* really is reflects not just linguistic confusion, but a deeper uncertainty on whether it belongs inside or outside of the Korean identity. It might be easy, for example, to attribute words such as “아이스크림” or “오케이” to their English origins, but what about when we mix both languages to make “갓생” or “화이팅”? Can we consider these to be blemishes and incorrect English appropriations when they are so foundationally Korean? These appropriations suggest a deeper creative system that cannot be reduced to mere incorrectness, prompting us to consider whether these words should be absorbed or rejected.

Many attribute Konglish’s widespread use to globalization: a product of the internet, a post-social media language boom. However, a deeper dive into the history of Korea’s linguistic landscape reveals just how deeply infused the English language has been since the peninsula opened its doors at the end of the 19th century: The integration of English has been gradual, nuanced, and interpreted by various groups over time. 

***The Opening of the Korean Peninsula***  
When Korea opened its doors at the end of the 19th century, some of the earliest outsiders to arrive in Korea were Americans, largely missionaries. These missionaries represent the first major shift in Korea’s relationships with foreign nations, marked by the 1883 opening of the first English school *Dongmunhak*, made to train Korean diplomats outbound to America. Around this time, English became the language of philosophy and politics and German the language of science; as early as the 1880s, we can see both used in popular academic publications such as 폐허 or *Ruins*, demonstrating a dedicated audience of English-reading intellectuals. English signified Korea’s step onto the global stage and the desire to engage in academic thought: scholarly identity for Korea became grounded in the adoption of English and German. Scientific concepts began to be written in Latin script and appropriated via *Hangul*, marking the shift from English as a separate entity to English as infused within the Korean language. 

***The Japanese Colonial Period***  
Then, Japan’s Protectorate State and occupation brought major (and grave) shifts in language policy with the institutionalization of the Japanese language. This moment in Korean history, however, is deceptively important for the English language, marking the emergence of what we now consider to be *Konglish* as English usage spiked up in two ways: colonially-influenced English and the English of liberation.

Firstly, Japan was slightly ahead on the English train: cosmopolitan English words had started being adopted in the name of urbanization. As Japan made efforts to “modernize” Korea, *Konglish* words were no longer purely academic and entered mainstream speech via the Koreanization of *Japanglish*. For example, archives from the *Donga-Ilbo* show a plethora of recurring columns focused entirely on language usage. “신어해설,” or *introduction of new words*, demonstrates some of the earliest instances of *Konglish*. In these articles, we see that as early as 1931, you might have heard young Koreans referring to a friend as a “쉬크 girl” (Chic girl), “이트맨” (it man). You might even hear phrases such as “三S시대” (3 “S” \[speed, sex, sports\] generation) as many “English” terms were imported from *Japanglish* and naturalized into Korean during this time. Although serving a colonial function, *Konglish* again represents a creative system embedded in Korea’s national identity: to be a “modern” colonial subject was to adopt “modern” English appropriations, often adapting them from *Japanglish* and reinventing terms that were relevant to life in Korea at the time.

Simultaneously, English began to possess properties of liberation. A novel phenomenon was that whereas English had historically been a colonial language across the globe, this was not the case for Korea. Although the Japanese colonial curriculum did enforce English, it was not the language of the colonizer. Instead, American schools served as religiously and therefore socially misaligned with colonial Japanese schools, and an important identity-forming relationship between language, religion, education, and politics emerged. English-based schools were American and Christian, fundamentally opposing the colonial Shintoism that was being enforced by the Japanese government at the time. We see this reflected in the prominence of English-using revolutionaries. Yun Ch'iho, for example—one of the most documented and prominent anti-colonial nationalists of the 1940s—chronicled his experiences of Japanese colonialism via an English diary. Activists such as Yun described the disparity between Japan’s treatment of Americans and Koreans, placing themselves in the middle of both as American-educated Korean missionaries. Here, their colonial grief was not just an alignment against Japan, but an alignment *with* America due to being educated in English. One’s own sense of self and of Korea as a nation was fundamentally impacted by the growing presence of American-aligning English-speaking Koreans. 

***The Continuation of English through American Military Presence***  
The English language and a growing *Konglish* interlanguage grew in importance both politically and socially, and English usage was incredibly dynamic and significant in the crafting of a post-colonial identity for many Koreans. The once niche subset of language was expanded and modified by incoming *Japanglish*, practiced by America-aligning missionaries and diplomats, and quickly compounded by the heavy presence of English-speaking US military following the Korean War. Before the term *Konglish* was even officially coined, a “bamboo English” emerged between American military personnel in Japan, soon spreading for use in Korea. Terms such as *switchee-switchee*—meaning “to change money”—were seen as the most efficient method of communication between the two groups. A *Konglish* prototype, this reimagining of the Korean and English language continued to serve as a creative exercise, and demonstrates how English became naturally integrated as *Konglish*: not erroneous attempts at language, but appropriations of valuable linguistic features used in meaningful communication and adapted over time for nuanced usage. 

This very system has continued to evolve over time according to the needs and desires of an ever-changing nation. From terms imported directly from *Japanglish* such as “오토바이”, to military slang, to ideas of “올백” hairstyles arriving from overseas, Korea has become increasingly globalized. From the rise of urban terminology to describe services such as “에이에스(A/S)” during the industrial boom of the late 20th century to internet phenomenon-describing phrases such as “프사” in more recent years, the endurance of *Konglish* is a testimony to the adaptiveness of both language and those that use it.

**So What Does This Mean?**  
This is to say that we cannot view the contemporary use of English in Korea as disconnected from a rich history of English usage nor innately incorrect. People had real uses for integrating English into language, and this need gradually led to the formation of a combined Korean-English. We can view modern iterations of *Konglish* as serving similar functions – seemingly trivial words such as “갓생” or “케바케” are not simply failures to achieve a ‘perfect’ English, but preservations of the same creative system that saw colonial subjects describe something as “에로” (an abbreviation of the Engligh “erotic”) or a local converse with an American soldier about a “rok” (the nickname for Korean soldiers, coming from the shortening of ROKA or *Republic of Korea Army*).

English has long served both practical and ideological functions. It became intertwined in modernization efforts, colonial resistance, and religion and education. To consider *Konglish* as merely incorrect and discourage it as disruptive to the idea of a “pure Korean” is to ignore the historical significance of English in Korea and the persisting practices that came from it. Although many instances may be from pieces of internet culture, *Konglish* is not an eruption of contemporary English usage in Korea. Rather, it relies on a system built on years of persistent expressions of agency and creativity. *Konglish* belongs to every merchant, early diplomat, and missionary school child in Korea that sought English as a tool for freedom of expression, a memory that we should not erase in defining *Konglish* as a failure to achieve traditionally Anglophone English.
